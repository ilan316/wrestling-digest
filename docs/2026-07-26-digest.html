Matt Sydal Returns to AEW

Matt Sydal is officially back in an AEW ring after nearly two years on the sidelines. Sydal wrestled on the July 25 episode of AEW Collision, teaming with Scorpio Sky and Dante Martin as SkyFlight against The Lethal Twist's Jay Lethal, Blake Christian and Lee Johnson.

The match marked Sydal's first AEW bout since 2024. He returned to the company in June, but fans had been waiting to see when he would finally step back between the ropes. Sydal looked right at home alongside Sky and Martin, displaying the veteran presence and athleticism that made him a valued member of the AEW roster before his extended absence.

However, the comeback did not end with a victory. Ricochet got involved during the closing stretch of the match, giving The Lethal Twist the opening they needed to steal the win. The interference immediately dragged Sydal back into AEW's ongoing drama and could point toward more trouble between SkyFlight and Ricochet moving forward, potentially setting up future encounters between these wrestlers.

Sydal may have come up short in his first match back, but getting through the bout was the bigger story. After nearly two years away from AEW competition, the veteran is healthy, active and back in the mix. His successful return to action signals that he is ready to contribute to the company's roster moving forward and could lead to significant storyline developments in the weeks ahead.

Ultimate Warrior and WCW History

How Ultimate Warrior's WCW Debut and Steve Austin's Missed Opportunity Exposed Behind-the-Scenes Turmoil

Eric Bischoff has provided fascinating insight into two critical moments from WCW's history that illuminate the challenges faced by the company during its later years. Both stories involve high-profile talent and showcase the internal dynamics that ultimately contributed to WCW's struggles during the Monday Night War.

The Ultimate Warrior's debut in WCW was supposed to give the company another massive weapon in its competition with WWE. However, according to Bischoff's account, the segment turned into a live television nightmare that helped transform Warrior's entire WCW run into a failed experiment. Bischoff explained during a conversation with Inside the Ropes that WCW had carefully planned Warrior's first major promo to last roughly five minutes. The company didn't use word-for-word scripts, but Warrior was given bullet points and the entire segment was walked through backstage before going live.

The five-minute limit wasn't merely a suggestion. WCW had commercial breaks to hit, and the entire live television show depended on talent staying close to their assigned time. As Bischoff explained, "It was supposed to be five minutes because we had commercials. They have this thing with TV—live TV. It's called commercial breaks. It's how you pay for everything." He continued, "We laid Warrior's promo out backstage. We walked through it because we didn't work off scripts, right? We just had bullet points. We had a basic idea of what we were going to do, and we had a pretty good idea of how long that should take."

Bischoff noted that experienced performers from that era had developed an internal clock that allowed them to finish segments within seconds of their assigned time. Warrior didn't come anywhere close. Bischoff reflected on the skill required: "Everybody, if they were motivated, could hit their times pretty close. You just had a clock inside your head. If somebody said to me, 'Eric, you've got three minutes. I want you to go out and sell me that glass of beer, and you've got three minutes to do it,' I'll do it in three minutes. Maybe 2:58, maybe 3:05, but I'm going to come within seconds of hitting my time. A lot of people who came up in that era of the '70s, '80s and early '90s had to develop that skill, or you wouldn't reach the top of your game."

Everything appeared to be settled backstage. Then Warrior entered the ring and simply kept talking. Bischoff compared standing there during the segment to being trapped inside a childhood nightmare. Warrior's promo blew past its five-minute limit and stretched to approximately 17 minutes, leaving Bischoff searching desperately for any way to make it stop. "We laid it all out backstage. Everybody's nodding their head like the Warriors. That's kind of what it was, right? Go back and watch the tape. I'm not lying. We go out, and I'm standing in the ring. It's just like a dream that you had when you were little, and you still remember that nightmare. I'm standing out there in the ring, and he's going on and on and on, and none of it makes any sense. It's the wackiest shit ever. Nuts."

The duration of the promo was staggering. "Seventeen minutes. It felt like it had to be 25. You must have seen the edited version, because the live version went on and on." Bischoff recalled the desperation he felt standing ringside. He even looked toward Hulk Hogan and wondered whether Hogan could simply knock Warrior out or create an unscheduled angle to end the promo. "I remember, literally, if you go back and look at it, I'm looking at Hulk like, 'What the fuck? Can you just knock him out? Can we just shoot an angle right here, right now? Throw everything out?' I mean, it was the most horrible feeling."

The disaster wasn't limited to the wrestlers standing inside the ring. Bischoff said WCW director Craig Leathers was normally impossible to rattle, but even he came out of the production truck looking completely destroyed after Warrior's promo. "Our director, his name is Craig Leathers. He's a super guy, a very talented director and always calm and cool. You could never rattle him, no matter what happened. This conversation happened shortly after Hogan arrived in WCW, before Monday Nitro existed, when WCW Saturday Night was still the company's flagship television show. Steve Austin came up to me, and this was right after Hulk Hogan got there, and said, 'Eric, I've got this great idea. I'm going to be Hulk Hogan's little brother. I said, 'Well, let me pass that on and we'll see where that goes.'"

Bischoff knew right away that Austin's idea faced one massive problem. Hogan preferred working with wrestlers he already trusted, which was a major reason Ric Flair played such an important role in bringing him to WCW. Hogan knew Flair would take care of him inside the ring and make him look like a star. Austin did not have that relationship with Hogan, and Bischoff didn't believe Hogan would get into a major program with him anytime soon.

"I knew in my mind, because one of the things when Hulk Hogan came in—you mentioned Ric Flair, and as I talked about—Hulk was very big on working with people that he trusted. These two stories serve as fascinating reminders of how different wrestling history might have been had key decisions played out differently during WCW's final years.

"By the time that came around, I had been doing – you know – I went from being a cowboy, to a rockabilly guy, to a Bad Ass, to 'hey, can we really pull this off?'
