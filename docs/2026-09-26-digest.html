🔥 John Morrison Compares Management Style of Tony Khan, Vince McMahon, and Triple H

Fresh off being crowned the inaugural PWR World Heavyweight Champion by defeating Big Damo, Dijak, and Jora Johl at Pro Wrestling Rebellion's Kranti event at the Talkatora Indoor Stadium in New Delhi, India, veteran wrestler John Morrison recently shared his unique insights on the management styles driving the professional wrestling industry today. Having worked extensively across different major promotions, Morrison offered a rare perspective comparing the operational control of Vince McMahon, Triple H, and Tony Khan.

When discussing the leadership differences between Vince McMahon and current WWE creative head Triple H, Morrison pointed to the massive shift in corporate structure brought on by WWE's parent company, TKO Group Holdings. According to Morrison, McMahon essentially operated without restrictions when it came to spending money or executing wild creative ideas on a whim. One difference between the two he sees is that Vince had no checks and balances. If Vince wanted, for example, an 18-wheeler full of milk with a pump that you would use to spray people, he could get it just like that. In contrast, Morrison believes Triple H does not possess that same level of unchecked control because he is under the TKO umbrella, meaning he cannot simply decide to spend a hundred thousand dollars on a truck right on the spot.

Morrison also drew direct comparisons between Vince McMahon and AEW President Tony Khan, noting striking similarities in their financial backgrounds and dedication to the craft. Following his own run in WWE that lasted from 2019 to 2021, Morrison joined AEW in 2022 and had the opportunity to observe Khan firsthand. Morrison stated that Tony Khan probably could have done anything he wanted with his wealth, which is the freedom you get with that amount of money, but because he is a genuine wrestling fan, he decided he wanted to work in the business of professional wrestling.

Morrison further praised Khan's relentless work ethic, pointing out that Khan works really hard and is personally present at all the television tapings. Ultimately, Morrison summed up his impression of the AEW boss by offering a memorable comparison regarding their temperaments and personal approaches, noting that he sometimes thinks Tony Khan is a lot like Vince except with a good heart, if you know what I mean.

Bryan Rose Hall of Fame Column

It is Hall of Fame season, and over the next couple of months, fans will likely read many articles across various outlets making the case for wrestlers and personalities on this year’s Wrestling Observer Hall of Fame ballot. Joining in to provide some perspective as a voter, I am sharing a partial list of the people I voted for last year, with future columns featuring more picks and new additions. For the record, two of my picks, Cody Rhodes and Sabu, made it in last year.

Junkyard Dog has a very strong case for induction simply because he set Mid-South on fire in the early 1980s, drawing big houses at the Superdome. He was easily the top babyface and houses were strong during his era. When he left Mid-South to join the World Wrestling Federation in 1984 it was a blow to the promotion, though they still managed to build stars for a few more years. JYD also did well initially in his WWF run, and was always a popular babyface. His career largely winded down after that, but his strong years were strong enough that I think he has a good chance of getting in eventually, as he was only off by a point or so last year, earning 58.6 percent out of the required 60 percent to get in. He only needed four more votes to make it.

The same standard applies to Kerry, Kevin, and David Von Erich. The Von Erichs were rock stars in Texas during the 1980s, drawing strong crowds all across the Lone Star State. When David passed away, Kerry’s match with Ric Flair for the National Wrestling Alliance title drew over 32,000 people at Texas Stadium. World Class Championship Wrestling was popular after David’s sudden death in 1984 and continued on for several more years. When people remember World Class in its heyday, however, it was Kerry, Kevin, and David.

Mark and Jay Briscoe represent another crucial selection. I cannot think of a team more important to independent wrestling in the 2000s and the 2010s than the Briscoes. They dominated Ring of Honor and created such a strong buzz for themselves both through their promo work and their athletic ability in the ring. Both were incredibly smart in knowing where the business was going and adapted as time moved on. Unfortunately, Jay’s controversial anti-gay remarks likely cost them opportunities elsewhere, and they were banned from appearing on All Elite Wrestling television by Warner Bros. when Tony Khan took over Ring of Honor in 2022. Jay’s untimely death the following year ended the tag team before they finally got their shot, ending their run with an incredible trilogy with FTR. The Briscoes legacy is undeniable, and influence matters in the Hall of Fame.

Matt and Jeff Hardy form another strong case. So many teams emulated the Hardy Boyz style once they became a hot act in the World Wrestling Federation in the late nineties, and their influence continues to be felt to this day. Their Tables, Ladders, and Chairs matches with the likes of Edge and Christian and the Dudley Boyz were the catalyst for those types of matches to proliferate not just in WWE, but all over pro wrestling. They are still a top-level tag team to this day, where they are arguably the top act in Total Nonstop Action.

Becky Lynch only garnering 28.3 percent in last year’s balloting stunned me. She should have been a first ballot candidate, no question. At one time, Lynch was the biggest star in WWE, drawing more than the men. Her popularity skyrocketed once WWE finally went with her and she became one of the first three women to headline WrestleMania alongside Ronda Rousey and Charlotte Flair, something that only a few years prior was simply unrealistic. She and Rousey, who I think can also strongly be argued for a Hall of Fame spot, changed how women were presented in WWE. It was one thing for Stephanie McMahon to come out on television one day and declare a moment was happening, but Lynch actually provided the organic movement women’s wrestling needed and changed the industry for the better. There is no better candidate on this list than her.
