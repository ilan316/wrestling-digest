Praise for Mercedes Mone as one of the all-time best American women's wrestlers

Mercedes Mone has sparked discussion about her potential Wrestling Observer Hall of Fame credentials following her AEW Women’s World Championship victory at All In. Mone defeated Willow Nightingale to capture the title, with longtime wrestling analyst Dave Meltzer subsequently awarding the match a five-star rating. Speaking on Wrestling Observer Radio, Meltzer discussed Mone’s résumé and highlighted the range of major championships and historic venues she has experienced throughout her career.

Meltzer noted that she would be the first woman ever to hold the WWE, CMLL, New Japan, IWGP, Strong, and AEW championships. He also pointed out that she became the first woman to compete in the Tokyo Dome, which very few women wrestlers have achieved, calling it the hardest venue for women wrestlers. Combined with competing at Madison Square Garden, Wembley, and Arena Mexico, these accomplishments serve as major resume points for Hall of Fame consideration.

For two years, King of the Ring was the fifth event on the WWE pay-per-view calendar until 1995 saw an explosion of In Your House events to bulk up the schedule. It is still a highly regarded tournament, with many winners going on to be future champions or stand out stars in their own right. WWE continues to tour the United Kingdom, with episodes of Raw and SmackDown occasionally emanating from major cities across the country. WWE have expanded into international territories with Clash and Backlash over past years, perhaps Insurrextion can be added to that slate.

Editorial on the importance and impact of independent wrestling

Professional wrestling is often discussed as if it begins when the television cameras turn on. Championships become important when they are defended on a national broadcast, wrestlers become worth knowing when they sign with major companies like WWE, AEW, or TNA, and crowds become meaningful only when attendance is large enough for a press release. That way of looking at wrestling ignores much of what makes the industry actually work. Independent wrestling matters because it is where professional wrestling remains closest to the people who create it, support it, and allow it to survive.

Over the past several months, extensive time has been spent covering independent wrestling events throughout Indiana, watching matches inside fairground buildings, event centers, and baseball stadiums. Audiences have varied in size, promotions have featured different resources, and wrestlers have been at dramatically different stages of their careers. What has never been seen is wrestling that feels insignificant simply because it is not being broadcast nationally.

At EMERGE 98 Back In The Saddle, fans frequently recognized who was about to appear before an entrance had even fully developed. The music would begin, the reaction would build, and the audience immediately understood the relationships between the wrestlers involved. That familiarity did not come from a national television package explaining who mattered; it came from months and years of wrestlers repeatedly appearing in front of the same community and earning a response directly.

At Grand Slam 2, professional wrestling occupied a baseball stadium in Kokomo, where Elijah Eros captured the Grand Slam Championship and Chris Owens fought through a No Disqualification main event in front of an audience gathered around something created specifically for that city. At Swinging for the Fences 2 in South Bend, the crowd’s connection with Tyler Matthews made his championship defense feel important before the opening bell even rang. The response was not manufactured by a marketing campaign telling the audience that Matthews was a hometown figure, because the people in the building already knew what he represented to them.

That deep connection is one of independent wrestling’s greatest strengths. While national promotions can use television time, video packages, commentary, and months of production to establish a performer, independent wrestlers frequently have only an entrance, a few minutes in the ring, and their own ability to make an audience care. They learn quickly whether their presentation works, hearing directly when a crowd reacts, when it loses interest, and when something unexpectedly connects. The audience is not simply consuming a finished product, but actively participating in the development of the people in front of it.

Independent wrestling is often described merely as a proving ground for wrestlers hoping to reach a larger company, and while it certainly performs that function, reducing the entire scene to a developmental system is dismissive. Not every meaningful wrestling career must conclude with a national contract. Some wrestlers build long careers traveling between regional promotions, some become champions in front of audiences that have watched them develop for years, some train the next generation, and some become dependable veterans capable of helping inexperienced wrestlers improve. Others simply create characters and stories that matter deeply to hundreds of people, even if millions never learn their names. That work still matters.

The independent scene also contains stories that cannot be understood by reading a match graphic. A results listing can tell readers whether Teagan Thorne won or lost a match, but it cannot explain that she was pronounced dead following a childhood car crash, survived an extensive recovery, and eventually transformed part of that experience into the foundation of her wrestling identity. Similarly, a championship listing cannot fully explain the true weight and personal significance of carrying those titles within these vibrant local communities.
