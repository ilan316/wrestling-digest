🔥 Ric Flair Denies WWE Return Rumors and Confirms AEW Contract

Ric Flair has shut down speculation that he could be heading back to WWE, calling the rumors both false and disrespectful to AEW and Tony Khan. Flair addressed the speculation in a post on social media, making it clear that despite maintaining a friendship with WWE Chief Content Officer Paul Triple H Levesque, he remains under contract with AEW.

Several outlets this past week ran comments attributed to Flair, teasing an appearance at the Royal Rumble and a reconciliation with Triple H. The old Flair quote stated that the road of life is going to lead him to you in Philadelphia, followed by coming to the Royal Rumble, seeing a lot of him now, being back in, and that him and Hunter hugged and made up. However, the quotes were not new. The comments were pulled from an episode of Booker T's Hall of Fame podcast, but that interview dated back to 2023. It resurfaced as part of a Labor Day compilation special, and outlets picked it up as if it were current, leading to the misunderstanding.

Flair addressed the situation directly in his statement. Number one, it is disrespectful. Number two, it is not true. He noted that he has always been friends with Hunter, that nothing has changed with their friendship, and that they both have mutual respect for each other. He also pointed to daughter Charlotte Flair's position in WWE, noting that she works for the company and he cheers her on.

According to Flair, a WWE return is not possible because he remains contracted to AEW and Tony Khan. The 16-time world champion praised Khan for allowing him the freedom to pursue his various outside business interests. Flair stated that he is very loyal to Khan as he allows freedom to pursue all business ventures and opportunities like Flair Spirits, all of my signings, WOOOOO Energy, and NASCAR. Flair closed by emphasizing that his relationship with Khan extends beyond business and asked for the WWE speculation to stop. Tony and I are personal friends, and he grew up watching me. I would never ever disrespect him. Please stop with the online rumors. I am happy where I am in my life.

AEW announced Flair's multi-year agreement in November 2023, with the deal also involving Wooooo Energy becoming the promotion's exclusive energy drink. Flair had debuted for AEW the previous week as Khan's surprise gift to Sting, with much of his initial run centered around Sting's retirement tour leading into Revolution. Flair has appeared only sporadically for AEW since then. A July report claiming he had parted ways with the promotion proved incorrect, and he later appeared on television to pay tribute to Steve McMichael. He was also announced for Blood and Guts alongside Ricky Steamboat but left the building before the broadcast due to a rotator cuff injury, while subsequent reports confirmed he remained under contract with AEW.

Flair's relationship with WWE has been more complicated in recent years. Earlier in the year, he publicly claimed WWE had banned him from WrestleMania and accused the company of contributing to the collapse of a clothing arrangement with Roots of Fight, though WWE did not publicly respond. During that same period, Flair spoke highly of Khan, telling an interviewer that the AEW President is one of the greatest people he has ever met while praising the opportunities and compensation he provides talent. Flair also acknowledged that his relationship with Triple H isn't what it once was, although he said he couldn't be angry with Levesque, crediting Triple H with helping rebuild his confidence through Evolution following his departure from WCW and recognizing the scrutiny Levesque faces in his current WWE role.

With those disclaimers out of the way, what I have to say now comes from a frustrated life-long wrestling fan: WWE needs to stop.

Without access to the conversations in WWE and TKO boardrooms that decide premium live event schedules, we will never know the motives and justifications for scheduling big shows on the same days as AEW. All we really have to go off of is what Nick Khan, Triple H, and other executives are telling us, but it is difficult not to try to fill in the space in between with our own perceptions. Whether they are doing it because they see AEW as a threat or because they want to put AEW out of business so they do not drive up the costs of talent contracts, one thing is clear. WWE and TKO is deliberately scheduling shows to counterprogram their biggest current competition. Pro-WWE people, at least those who are not outright denying that counterprogramming is a thing, might say that is just business. If AEW wants to be mentioned in the same breath as the company that has been on top of the industry for decades, they should actually be happy that WWE is counterprogramming them. It is a sign that WWE actually views them as a threat to knock them off their perch as the number one wrestling company in the country, and perhaps the entire world. In fact, at least one of AEW's top stars subscribes to that line of thinking. While I do not want to speak for the AEW side of things too much, there are two prevailing thought processes that I have seen from our community and elsewhere on the wrestle web. Typically, it is either disdain for the evil fed's predatory booking or it is a confident bring-it-on attitude from people who love AEW and do not really care what WWE is doing.

Essentially, WWE has gotten into the habit of using its secondary brands, especially NXT, as sacrificial lambs to try to take a chunk out of AEW's audience. Look at it this way, using August 30th's full day of wrestling as an example: People who planned to watch All In were never going to watch NXT Heatwave instead. People who would potentially watch both companies were probably going to prioritize All In, with it being AEW's biggest show of the year. Zuffa's Potential Expansion into Professional Boxing

Can Zuffa Rebuild Boxing?

Could professional boxing have an opportunity to re-establish itself in the United States? Stretching as far back as the closed-circuit, the predecessor to pay-per-view that was instrumental in the national expansion of pro wrestling years later, era of the sweet science, boxing had a way to monetize itself on a major scale. Basically, the sport was on the forefront of mass distribution, regardless of what the form of media was at the time. The Rumble in The Jungle saw fans flock to those previously mentioned closed-circuit location. When HBO launched as a form of premium television when basic cable was still in its formative stages, the ability to watch live boxing cards, along with movies that had recent theatrical runs, was one of the main selling points for consumers to pay a little extra. As time went on, Mike Tyson became a pay-per-view exclusive, but there was still a portion of his prime that aired on the subscription networks. The same can be said for some of the biggest names in the sport. Furthermore, when technology advanced to the point that customers could order the events directly at home, boxing often generated a few million buys a year between the handful of major bouts that were broadcasted. Oscar De La Hoya, Evander Holyfield, and Lennox Lewis generated four million buys between four pay-per-views that were held in 1999.

As we know, the political red tape, especially as networks expanded and there was more money on the table, almost completely destroyed the sport. For decades, the top fighters clashed in classic battles that made the sport not only as popular as it was, but a part of pop culture in America at different points in time. In the late-90s, there were almost weekly cards on HBO, and ESPN had the Friday Nights series to showcase the up-and-coming fighters as well. The cards every few weeks on the linear HBO channel were used to promote signature pay-per-view events, and it kept the business model strong. At the same time, legendary slug fests such as the Gatti-Ward trilogy maintained the selling point for the subscription channel itself, along with newer dramas like The Sopranos and The Wire.

The bottom line is, when the top fighters competed against each other, the industry of boxing was much healthier. By the mid-2000s, that changed and it became more about promoters that wanted to protect their golden goose rather than draw the biggest money possible. Floyd Mayweather, a masterful defensive technician, was in position to be the top name in the sport, specially after the peak heavyweights of the late-90s had retired, and he secured a career-defining win against the previously mentioned De La Hoya in 2007.

Unfortunately, Floyd became more about protecting his brand than fighting the best competition possible. He fought a 23-year-old Canelo Alvarez before the Mexican superstar had the experience to truly be a threat to his unblemished record. Neither Victor Ortiz nor Robert Guerrero had any business being in the ring with Mayweather, and the same could be said for Andre Berto later in Floyd’s career. Marcos Maidana was competitive against him, which was proven by the debatable split decision that Floyd won on the score cards before he won a unanimous decision in the rematch. Essentially, Mayweather’s career devolved into glorified exhibitions, Showtime actually lost money on the contract that he had with the network when buy rates didn’t cover his hefty guarantee. He strategically fought a diminished Manny Pacquiao five years too late, and the super fight that the public waited years to see was woefully underwhelming. HBO and Showtime, networks that were major distribution channels for the sport, got out of the boxing business almost decade ago when the economics were no longer profitable for their platforms. Floyd vs. Conor McGregor was the last money grab of Mayweather’s pro boxing career, setting a pay-per-view record for the novelty alone, but the one-sided bout wasn’t sold on the competition.

The reason WWE is de-emphasizing tag teams is the same reason that I publicly stated I was doing in 1994: it doesn't make financial sense.
