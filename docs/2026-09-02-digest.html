Nick Comoroto Recalls Spending Years on WWE Waitlist Before Signing With AEW

While the tryout went well enough to put Comoroto on WWE's radar, an actual contract did not immediately follow.

Comoroto explained that Brisco put him with a couple of others and got him put on a to be signed list. He remained on that list for about two and a half years before they finally signed him. Comoroto ultimately signed with WWE in 2019 and was assigned to NXT, though most of his work came at live events. Following his departure from WWE, Comoroto later joined AEW and has also made numerous appearances for ROH.

Tony Khan Plans Headline Inductee for AEW Hall of Fame

Ahead of July’s AEW Redemption pay-per-view, Tony Khan told the Battleground Podcast that he was interested in starting up a Hall of Fame in AEW one day. With the company now seven years on from its inception, it may soon be time for Khan to consider pulling the trigger and honoring some of the legendary figures that have helped AEW become what it is today. Here are three potential candidates to be the headline inductee of the first AEW Hall of Fame class.

Chris Jericho
While his presence in AEW has been bemoaned by some fans in recent years, Chris Jericho’s legacy in pro wrestling and AEW in particular is undisputed. Jericho was AEW’s first major signing back in 2019, and his presence alone will have helped the startup company attract viewers due to his past work on the biggest stage in WWE. Jericho was also the very first AEW World Champion, defeating Hangman Adam Page for the gold at All Out 2019. However, even after losing the gold the following year at Revolution, Jericho remained a focal point on TV with major storylines alongside MJF, Kenny Omega and many others. Given that AEW may have found it a lot more difficult to get off the ground had Jericho not been there, and his work in elevating other talent since, Jericho would make for a worthy first headline inductee into the AEW Hall of Fame.

Mr. Brodie Lee
Mr. Brodie Lee arrived in AEW in 2020 after being granted his release from WWE at the end of 2019, and was immediately involved in a major angle when he was revealed as The Exalted One leading the mysterious Dark Order group, who had been terrorizing AEW for months beforehand. By the summer of 2020, Lee dethroned Cody Rhodes to become the second-ever TNT Champion, and would hold the title until losing it back to Cody two months later in a dog collar match. This tragically proved to be Lee’s final match, as in December of 2020, he passed away at the age of 41. However, his legacy lives on in AEW. Not only did the company put on a wonderful tribute show to Lee’s life following his passing, but they also added his son Brodie Lee Jr. to their roster, with the 14-year-old making his in-ring debut in GCW this past April. Considering his in-ring legacy and impact on so many talent in AEW and the wider wrestling world, Mr. Brodie Lee is certainly a worthy first headline inductee in the AEW Hall of Fame.

Sting
A two-time Hall of Famer before he ever stepped foot in AEW, Sting would certainly be among the favorites to be inducted first, should AEW start up a Hall of Fame. After a massively disappointing WWE run which saw him lose his one and only WrestleMania match to Triple H, Sting’s career was done justice by Tony Khan. The Stinger was given a true final run, where he consistently surprised fans with what he was willing to do inside the ring, namely jumping off of high things. Still popping up for sporadic appearances, most recently at AEW All In London 2026, Sting is more than worthy of receiving a third Hall of Famer ring, alongside his TNA and WWE honors.

Analysis of Frozen Contracts in Professional Wrestling

How Frozen Contracts Actually Work in Pro Wrestling

A wrestling contract can reach the expiration date everyone had circled and still keep going. The wrestler does not sign a new deal, but one clause can push free agency back by months or even longer.

It is called freezing a contract, although promotions also describe it as adding injury time. WWE, AEW and TNA have all used versions of the practice. The process begins with the original contract. That agreement can give the promotion the right to pause the wrestler’s contracted service time when they are unavailable because of an injury, pregnancy or another covered absence.

The promotion then counts the time the wrestler missed and decides whether to add it to the end of the deal. If a wrestler’s contract is scheduled to expire on July 1 but they missed seven months, the company can push that expiration date into February of the following year. The wrestler does not need to sign another contract because the right to add that time was included in the deal they already signed. The wrestler continues receiving the pay guaranteed under the contract. Their salary is not shut off during the freeze and restarted when they return.

What stops is the clock leading to free agency. While the added time is being served, the wrestler remains under contract. They cannot sign with another major promotion, and outside bookings or merchandise deals remain subject to the restrictions in their agreement.

The company can bring the wrestler back during that extension, but it does not have to use them on television. A wrestler can remain at home, continue getting paid and still be blocked from leaving until every added day is finished. The extension is not always automatic. The contract gives the promotion the option to add time, but the company can decide how much time qualifies or whether it wants to exercise that option at all.

Carmella gave a firsthand explanation of WWE’s process after her contract expired in February 2025. WWE froze her deal during pregnancy, continued paying her and initially gave her a set amount of time away. Carmella believed WWE would add her entire absence once she was medically able to return. Instead, WWE reinstated her contract seven weeks after she gave birth, even though she had not returned to work and was dealing with nerve damage in her foot. The remaining time then continued running until the deal expired.

Her situation showed the other side of the system: a promotion can freeze a contract, but it can also start the clock again or decide not to claim every possible month. AEW uses the same basic mechanism. Adam Copeland said time was added to his deal for the broken leg that kept him out of action and for the time he missed while filming The Beekeeper 2 and Percy Jackson.

Danhausen’s AEW contract became a more controversial example. His deal was expected to expire during the summer of 2025, but AEW added injury time and pushed it to February 28, 2026. He remained signed and paid even though he wanted out if the company had no plans to use him.

That is how frozen contracts work across pro wrestling. The company counts qualifying time away, moves the expiration date and continues paying the wrestler while keeping the original agreement alive. The exact language, amount of time and reason for the freeze can change from one contract to another.

BJ Bethel went silent on Twitter at the exact moment his major broadcasting and wrestling industry story blew up in his face. Now he has resurfaced on an alternative social media platform with an explanation, but the timing is awfully convenient.

Bethel has not posted on his Twitter account since the fallout from his claim that ESPN had already decided not to renew its five-year, $1.625 billion agreement with WWE. ESPN directly denied the story on August 25, and his publishing outlet retracted it before firing Bethel one day later.

Bethel is now responding to people on Bluesky, where he insists he still stands behind the story ESPN called false. He also claims attempted hacking is the real reason he stopped posting on Twitter, stating that he hasn't been posting because people have been trying to hack him since Tuesday.

Here is the problem: Tuesday was August 25, which was the same day ESPN publicly killed his story by stating there is no truth to the report that they had decided not to renew. Bethel didn't say his account was successfully hacked. He didn't say he lost access, was locked out, or had posts deleted. He only claimed people had been trying to get into it, and those are two very different things.

In his Bluesky response, Bethel offered no screenshots of security warnings, no login alerts, and no explanation for how attempted hacking prevented him from posting. He also didn't explain why the alleged attempts began on the exact day ESPN denied his biggest story, right before his employer fired him the next day.

His former media outlet retracted Bethel's story in full and admitted that ESPN and WWE were not given a proper opportunity to respond before publication. The organization then announced that it had cut ties with Bethel and brought in an outside partner to review its verification process. Management released a statement noting that they published a report on the WWE and ESPN relationship, are retracting that story in its entirety, and acknowledging that before a story like this runs, the subject deserves a direct chance to respond, which is a step that wasn't taken to the appropriate standard. They also confirmed they parted ways with Mr. Bethel, had their verification procedures reviewed by an outside partner, and updated their process as a result.

The timeline of events remains brutal. ESPN denied the story on Tuesday, Bethel claims hacking attempts started Tuesday, and his outlet fired him on Wednesday. His Twitter account then went quiet while he eventually popped up elsewhere claiming the silence had nothing to do with the public disaster unfolding around him.

Could someone have tried to access his account? Sure. Bethel hasn't shown anything proving otherwise or proving that it actually happened. What he has done instead is blame an unverified hacking attempt for disappearing from Twitter at the most convenient possible moment.

He is also still standing behind a story that ESPN called false and his own former outlet deleted in its entirety. Bethel may want people to believe hackers chased him off Twitter, but without any supporting evidence, the timing looks less like a cyberattack and much more like someone avoiding the platform where his professional narrative just collapsed.

🔄 המשך Matt Cardona's Future in Professional Wrestling

Beyond his current injury recovery, Cardona's career serves as a masterclass in post-WWE reinvention.

When mass cuts hit the industry during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, released performers faced a grim landscape with limited independent bookings. Wrestlers typically handle their departures in a few distinct ways. Some simply rely on their past WWE television exposure to work independent dates without changing their presentation, much like Heath Slater. Others, like No Way Jose, struggle on the independent scene because their gimmicks rely too heavily on WWE production bells and whistles. A select few attempt major reinventions, yielding mixed results, as seen with Braun Strowman's early post-WWE struggles compared to Cody Rhodes evolving into a top-tier industry leader.

Cardona took the path of complete transformation after nearly fifteen years under a WWE contract. For the vast majority of that tenure, he embodied the quintessential WWE developmental system. He was trained to follow the corporate playbook by adopting a clean presentation, an easy-to-understand gimmick, marketable catchphrases, and a safe in-ring style designed to protect a wrestler's longevity.

However, instead of fading into obscurity with his old Zack Ryder identity, he shattered that conservative game plan. He completely reshaped public perception by trading the safe style for the gritty independent circuit, famously reinventing himself and even embracing hardcore death matches in Game Changer Wrestling. That relentless hustle successfully transformed a traditional television wrestler into one of the most adaptable and lucrative independent operators of the modern era.
