🔥 Dustin Rhodes Social Media Controversy

DUSTIN RHODES SOCIAL MEDIA CONTROVERSY

The controversy began on August 18, 2026, when Dustin Rhodes, an AEW veteran currently sidelined from in-ring competition, responded to a social media post on X featuring a woman described as "Unapologetically ULTRA-MAGA Patriot" known as "Just Jen Rx." The August 14 post included a bathroom selfie of the influencer wearing a t-shirt depicting a women's restroom sign above the words "come and take it" and posing to emphasize her chest and a firearm strapped around her hips. The caption read: "Hey ISLAM, come at take it. I dare ya."

Rhodes replied to the post with a heart emoji and a "rock on" hand sign, seemingly endorsing the content. The "rock on" emoji featured the hand gesture commonly associated with the University of Texas Longhorns, where Rhodes has deep ties. The 57-year-old was born in Dallas and grew up in Austin, and previously named his Ring of Honor stable "The Sons of Texas."

The response quickly generated backlash from fans and wrestlers alike who interpreted Rhodes' emoji reaction as support for the post's Islamophobic and transphobic messaging. Fans immediately began questioning why Rhodes would publicly endorse such content, with some noting his history of working alongside Muslim wrestlers. One fan brought up his collaborations with Muslim coworkers, while another expressed particular disappointment, writing: "Man this sucks, as a muslim i was a big fan of you as goldust what a shame."

The controversy eventually reached AEW President Tony Khan, with fans tagging him directly and demanding action. Some called on Khan to keep Rhodes away from the company and its upcoming Wembley appearance, with one fan writing: "@TonyKhan keep this washed up bigot off aew pls and pls don't let him be at Wembley. I'd seriously consider boycotting aew." Another fan also tagged Khan, interpreting Rhodes' response as endorsing gun violence while noting Khan's visibly Muslim last name.

TNA Wrestling star Mustafa Ali, who is Muslim and has spoken extensively throughout his career about the harm done by Islamophobic stereotypes, directly addressed Rhodes on X. Ali wrote: "I respect you and your family immensely. You showing support to something like this is really disappointing."

The response prompted Rhodes to delete his original interaction and immediately issue an explanation. Rhodes claimed he had not read the caption attached to the post and had simply reacted because he liked the firearm shown in the photo. In his first response to the controversy, Rhodes wrote on a screenshot shared by NoDQ.com: "Sorry, didn't read the caption. Just liked the firearm. My apologies."

Rhodes then issued a more detailed apology directly addressing Ali: "My apologies @MustafaAli_X. I am not that person. Genuinely like the photo. Did not read caption and don't agree with that at all." In a separate statement, he expanded further: "My apologies for liking a firearm pic. Did not not read the caption and that is my bad. I by no means, and you all know, I am not a racist person. We all bleed red. Truthfully liked the firearm in picture, nothing more. I sincerely apologize."

Rhodes also emphasized his support for the transgender community. According to multiple reports, Rhodes has a transgender stepson and has long been a vocal supporter of trans rights. When addressing the situation, he stressed: "Yall can think what you want, but I am or have selective reading. Not a lie. Once again, i am sorry for not reading the post. I am 110% against what she said after looking. I am being honest."

When one fan suggested that the post's implications were about religious bigotry and anti-trans messaging rather than racism, and recommended Rhodes issue a specific apology to Muslims and the trans community, Rhodes initially declined. He responded: "I don't need to because I'm not a racist person. Like I said I didn't read. I liked the picture that is all and everybody knows that I stand behind Tran people."

Mustafa Ali accepted Rhodes' apology while emphasizing the broader concern about normalizing such rhetoric. Ali responded: "I appreciate the apology, please be careful about anti-whatever rhetoric posts. Normalizing it is dangerous for all of us. Be well, Dustin." Ali's measured acceptance appeared to help de-escalate the immediate situation, with the exchange ending on a conciliatory note.

Despite the apology, Rhodes expressed frustration with the backlash and social media in general. He posted: "Think I'm gonna quit this app. No matter how many people i help, or how many i inspire, there is always so many trying to break you down. Tired." Following this statement, Rhodes made his X account private, effectively removing his posts from public view and limiting his interactions on the platform.

Rhodes subsequently indicated he may leave X altogether in favor of other social media platforms, directing followers to his Instagram account instead. The entire incident, which began with two emojis, became one of wrestling's biggest social media talking points, serving as another reminder of how quickly a single click can escalate into significant controversy.

Regarding Rhodes' current status in AEW, the veteran underwent double knee replacement surgery in summer 2025 and has been out of action for more than a year. He previously noted in June that he was close to being cleared to wrestle and had already returned to his backstage coaching role. 🔥 Paul Wight Commentary on Wrestling Industry

Paul Wight Speaks Out Against Wrestling's "Paying Your Dues" Culture

Paul Wight has become one of the most vocal voices in the ongoing debate about whether young wrestlers need to "pay their dues" in the professional wrestling industry. Speaking to Inside the Ring, the current AEW star made his position abundantly clear, using strong language to express his frustration with what he sees as an outdated and harmful practice.

The debate was reignited when Avery Styles, the teenage son of wrestling legend AJ Styles, was publicly criticized by veteran wrestler Arik Cannon for not helping set up chairs at a GCW event. Cannon claimed that Styles was "skirting away from helping with a single thing," implying that the young wrestler was failing to fulfill his responsibility to "pay his dues." Avery's father quickly jumped to his defense, as did other industry figures including Cody Rhodes and Lexis King. Now Wight has added his considerable voice to the conversation, arguing that the entire concept is fundamentally flawed.

Wight's Core Argument Against the "Dues" Mentality

When asked directly about paying dues, Wight did not mince words. "F*** that bulls***," he began, before elaborating on his philosophy. "Paying your dues to me is an old-timer's way of intimidating and bullying and f***ing with the younger talent for no goddamn reason."

However, Wight made clear that his objection to the dues-paying concept does not mean he believes young wrestlers should arrive in the business with an entitled attitude. Instead, he emphasized that respect and a proper demeanor are essential. "If a talent comes in respectful with a good attitude, don't come in like you own the place, don't have an attitude, but why should you take heat because your athleticism, your look, or what opportunities you've been given, you've been successful on?" he asked.

The core of Wight's argument centers on what he sees as an illogical punishment system. "Why should someone hold that against you? Because you didn't drive for $35 a night and you didn't set up some broken, torn-ass-down ring and you didn't struggle for all this stuff? Okay, why punish somebody for that?" he posed rhetorically. He views the practice as veterans using their hardship as a justification for making the next generation endure unnecessary suffering.

Wight's Personal Experience

Wight's perspective is deeply informed by his own entry into professional wrestling. When he broke into WCW at just 22 years old, his first match was against none other than Hulk Hogan for the WCW World Heavyweight Championship on pay-per-view. This immediate push to the top generated significant resentment from veterans who had paid their dues through years of struggling on the independent circuit.

"Everybody was so mad at me because my first match was against Hulk Hogan. I was 22 years old. Is that my fault?" Wight reflected. "Should I have gone to independents? Should I have learned how people lie, manipulate and bulls*** in smaller leagues and promoters don't pay you and all these other stories that all these other old-timers went through that made them seasoned? To me, that's bitter sauce."

Wight views the resistance he faced as rooted in bitterness rather than legitimate business concerns. He refused to accept then, and refuses to accept now, the idea that younger talent should be punished for having advantages that previous generations did not possess.

A New Vision for Mentorship

Rather than perpetuating the cycle of hazing, Wight advocates for a different approach where veterans use their experience to help the next generation avoid pitfalls. "I don't like that, punishing a younger talent, because that's not what our business is about," he stated. "Our business should be about inspiring younger talent, helping them so they don't make mistakes, helping them avoid pitfalls."

That said, Wight is not suggesting that veterans should endlessly tolerate disrespect or unwillingness to listen. He operates under what he calls a "three-strike rule." "I have a three-strike rule. I will tell you something, give you advice three times. If you don't listen to it, I'm done. I'm not going to waste my breath. You can figure it out yourself," he explained.

When working with younger talent, Wight also emphasized the importance of honesty. Veterans should tell young wrestlers the truth about their capabilities and trajectory, even if it's not what they want to hear, rather than simply providing empty encouragement.

The Evolution of the Industry

Wight further argued that the very concept of paying dues has become outdated as the wrestling industry has evolved. He drew a parallel between old and new methods of self-promotion. "What are paying the dues now? You know, the business has changed so much. These kids have brands now with social media and they understand TikTok and Instagram and Twitter. They are smoking it on that and building their brand and extending their brand," he noted.

He challenged the logic behind the distinction between traditional and modern methods. "What's the difference between them doing that or riding up and down the road sticking 8x10 sheets of paper to a telephone pole to let everybody know that everybody's wrestling at the armory that night?" Wight asked. Both approaches serve the same purpose of promotion and brand-building, yet one is accepted as legitimate dues-paying while the other is sometimes dismissed.

"I think it all depends on the talent, and I think it depends on their attitude toward the business," Wight concluded on this point.

The Counterattack from Sam Gradwell

However, not everyone in the wrestling community has embraced Wight's perspective. Former NXT UK wrestler Sam Gradwell took to social media to challenge Wight's credibility on the subject of bullying young talent. Gradwell, who competed for WWE from 2017 to 2022, recalled his own negative experience with Wight during a WWE tryout.

"Does Paul remember what a nasty intimidating piece of s*** he was to my group of extra/tryout talent? Shouting over us that we didn't deserve to be paid and that we'd never be in the wrestling business because of a U.S paperwork misunderstanding on WWE's part, not ours. F*** off," Gradwell wrote.

Gradwell's group at that tryout included notable names such as Joe Hendry, Nathan Cruz, Joe Coffey, Mark Coffey, and Zack Gibson. The allegation suggests that Wight may have engaged in the very behavior he is now criticizing, creating a significant credibility issue for his current stance. Gradwell's comments received considerable pushback from online wrestling fans, many of whom trolled him in the comment section rather than engaging with the substance of his claim.

Wight's Broader Industry Commentary

Beyond the dues-paying debate, Wight has made other significant observations about the wrestling industry. In separate comments, he discussed the structural advantages WWE has accumulated over decades and how those advantages have affected AEW's ability to grow.

Wight spent more than 20 years helping build WWE and witnessed firsthand the company's evolution through the territories, the Attitude Era, business downturns, and its eventual global dominance. He believes AEW, which has only been around for approximately seven years, deserves a different measuring stick.

"You can't really compare logistically with a company that's had 40 years of dominance because they didn't have any competition, to somebody that where competition was so thoroughly snuffed out," he argued. Wight also claimed that WWE's dominance has created concrete obstacles for AEW beyond just ratings comparisons. He alleged that certain buildings that AEW could fill have been unavailable due to contractual agreements between WWE and those venues.

According to Wight, WWE's agreements with venues have included provisions preventing other wrestling events from taking place within 90 days before or after WWE runs that facility. "There were buildings that we couldn't wrestle in in AEW that we could fill because there were contract agreements between them and WWE, there was no wrestling 90 days before, no wrestling 90 days after," he stated.

Wight's Own Journey in AEW

Wight's current perspective on mentorship and helping younger talent comes from his experience in AEW, where he has faced significant physical challenges. Over six years with the company, he has undergone multiple surgeries, including knee replacements and hip replacements, which have limited his in-ring work considerably.

When reflecting on his situation, Wight demonstrated surprising self-awareness. "I would have fired me three years ago if you want to be honest about it," he admitted, comparing his tenure to how things might have gone under different circumstances. 🔥 Jim Johnston WWE Music Composer Future

Jim Johnston, the former WWE music composer who spent nearly three decades with the promotion before departing in 2017, recently opened up about his strained relationship with Triple H and his prospects of returning to create entrance themes for the company.

During an appearance on the All Access Entertainment podcast, Johnston reflected on his work with WWE and his perception of why he has not been asked to return despite his legendary status in the industry. Johnston's resume includes some of the most recognizable wrestling themes ever created, including those for Steve Austin, The Rock, The Undertaker, D-Generation X, and countless other WWE superstars. Johnston addressed Triple H's feelings toward him directly, suggesting that the WWE Chief Content Officer does not hold him in high regard. According to Johnston, Triple H wants to maintain the perception that Motorhead wrote his entrance themes, particularly "The Game" and "King of Kings," despite Johnston's significant contributions to their creation. Johnston believes this perception issue stems from Triple H's transition from an in-ring performer to a behind-the-scenes executive role within WWE.

"I think Hunter wants to maintain that Motorhead wrote those songs. I don't think he's a very big fan. I think, when he was coming out of wrestling into more of a behind-the-scenes role, he clearly wanted his own guys and he perceived me as Vince's guy. I think that's why I'm not being asked to write themes anymore," Johnston explained.

When asked whether he would be interested in composing entrance music again, Johnston expressed willingness but acknowledged the reality of his situation. He stated that someone in a position of authority must extend an invitation, and he believes the likelihood of that happening is extremely remote.

"I'd be happy to do it, but you have to be asked. The chances that Hunter is going to ask are close to zero. The chances that the WWE Music Group is going to call me up are zero, and the chances that the guy who is currently doing themes for WWE is going to call me up are zero. Why would you invite me in or invite somebody else in?" Johnston asked rhetorically.

Johnston further elaborated on the structural barriers preventing his return, suggesting that WWE's current music operation has become consolidated under the control of those who view him unfavorably. He expressed confidence in his abilities based on his decades of proven success but acknowledged that factors beyond his control are preventing opportunities.

Interestingly, Johnston revealed that he remains creatively active despite his departure from WWE. He spends time in his studio daily, regularly playing guitar and developing theme ideas that he believes could work well for wrestlers.

"I still come up with theme ideas all the time, you know, just me toying around on the guitar and like, that's a really good theme idea," he said.

Johnston's frustration with the situation extends beyond simply not receiving offers from WWE. He has been critical of the quality of current WWE entrance music, believing that his proven track record should speak for itself.

"Without being cocky about it, I'm fairly confident that I've proved that I can do this. And the biggest player, WWE, has really crappy music. So, it just tells me that there's just petty crap in the way here," Johnston stated.

Another concerning development Johnston mentioned is that he does not receive inquiries from WWE talent, despite occasionally hearing from wrestlers outside the company. Johnston suspects that WWE performers may have been instructed not to contact him regarding music composition.

"I occasionally get contacted by a wrestler, but never from WWE, and I think that's because they've been told not to," Johnston suggested.

Adding to this, Johnston mentioned that some WWE wrestlers have attempted to secure their own entrance music from outside sources, offering to pay for custom compositions themselves, only to have their requests denied by management. This suggests that WWE maintains strict control over all entrance music decisions, further limiting Johnston's prospects of returning.

"Guys have told me they tell the powers that be, 'I'll find someone. I'll pay for it. You know, no responsibility on your part. Can I do it?' 'No. This is your music. Boom,'" Johnston recounted.

However, Johnston acknowledged that he cannot definitively determine where all decisions originate within WWE's corporate structure. He questioned whether top-level WWE and TKO executives are even aware of every decision being made further down the corporate hierarchy.

Johnston's comments represent a candid assessment of his future prospects with WWE. While he remains open to opportunities and confident in his abilities, he has made clear that he does not expect Triple H to extend an invitation for him to return to compose entrance themes. His decades-long tenure with WWE and the iconic status of his work remain overshadowed by what appears to be a personal and professional distance between Johnston and the company's current leadership.

Blake Monroe Stardom Wrestling Experience

Blake Monroe reveals how her wrestling experience in Japan fundamentally shaped her character development and performance style. During an appearance on The Nikki & Brie Show, Monroe told Nikki Bella that her time spent wrestling in Stardom was instrumental in creating "The Glamour."

Monroe explained that not speaking Japanese forced her to develop an entirely different approach to character work. Since she couldn't rely on promos, which are traditionally one of the most important tools for connecting with audiences, she had to find alternative methods to make her character resonate. She said, "I couldn't speak Japanese. I learned phrases, and my last match I did actually learn a promo. It's on YouTube. I did a full promo in Japanese. I spent days learning it. But I couldn't speak, and as we know, promos, especially in WWE, are the biggest part of how you're going to make people like you. Wrestling is one thing, but I feel like you have to have something where people hate you, like you, relate to you, whatever it is. I couldn't do that in Japan, so I had to learn how to exude that in my body, in the way I dress, in all my mannerisms."

The language barrier became a blessing in disguise for Monroe's character evolution. Without the ability to communicate verbally with audiences, she intensified every other aspect of her performance. She stated, "And it really made me dial it up to 100. I never really undialed it." This commitment to maximizing her physical presence and visual presentation became the foundation of her character work going forward.

Monroe's Stardom tenure was notable, beginning with her debut in December 2022 alongside Mina Shirakawa and Xia Brookside as part of Club Venus. Her success continued throughout 2023 when she and Shirakawa won the Goddesses of Stardom titles on June 25, 2023, by defeating Ami Sohrei and MIRAI. Though she and Shirakawa lost those titles to Natsupoi and Saori Anou that August, Monroe remained active in the promotion. She competed in the 2023 5 Star Grand Prix, finishing with six points in her block and not advancing to the final stage of the tournament.

Monroe credited the transition to American wrestling with further developing her character. She explained, "Then coming to America and wrestling, I've just been fortunate as well to work with people who have that charisma, and it helps you bring it out. So now I feel like I'm there. I've developed it. Whereas four years ago, I don't think I would have had that character." Working alongside charismatic performers in America provided additional refinement to the foundation she had built in Japan.

Monroe's move to American wrestling came on January 3, 2024, when she made her in-ring debut on an episode of AEW Dynamite, defeating Queen Aminata. Her journey from the Japanese wrestling scene to American prominence demonstrates how limitations in one environment can become strengths in another, ultimately shaping a performer's entire career trajectory.

🔄 המשך NJPW G1 Climax 2026 Coverage

Following the G1 Climax 36 finals, Dave Meltzer offered insight into what made the tournament work so well. According to Meltzer on Wrestling Observer Radio, the key was New Japan's decision to finally push new performers into the spotlight rather than falling back on established names like Hirooki Goto, Sabre, or Shingo Takagi. "They really booked this tournament well," Meltzer said. "And the key was...this is where Gedo had been so slow on the draw, painfully slow. But I think that he finally figured out that to turn this thing around, you got to get these guys over, you know, some new people over. And the people were dying for it. They wanted somebody who had never won the G1 in the finals, and had never been IWGP champion in the finals."

Bryan Alvarez offered particularly high praise for the technical execution of the main event itself. Describing it as one of his favorite matches of the year, Alvarez highlighted how Oiwa and Uemura built drama through submission holds, particularly Oiwa's Big Lock headlock. He compared the emotional investment to watching hardcore AEW matches with extreme spots, saying viewers found themselves genuinely grimacing as Oiwa cranked the headlock in the final stretch. "They built this up so good," Alvarez said. "By the time this match was ending, I was like, 'God, this headlock, man. Just let it go. Just give up, dude.' I mean, they did such an incredible job with this headlock and with this wristlock in this match."

However, not everyone is confident NJPW can sustain this momentum. One wrestling observer noted that while the G1 booking was sound, the promotion has a troubling track record of squandering goodwill. Earlier in 2026, New Japan started the year strong with Wrestle Kingdom 19, which featured Tsuji winning the IWGP World Heavyweight Championship, Aaron Wolf's impressive debut, and Tanahashi's retirement match. Yet by the time the G1 began just a month later, that optimism had evaporated due to Wolf's booking being derailed by House of Torture feuds and Tsuji's inconsistent title reign. The critic warned that without consistent follow-through, the current goodwill from the G1 finals could similarly dissipate before Wrestle Kingdom.

🔄 המשך GCW Drake Wuertz Controversy

LAUDERDALE ACKNOWLEDGES WUERTZ'S CONTROVERSIAL PAST WHILE DEFENDING BOOKING

In addressing the backlash to Drake Wuertz's appearance at GCW Homecoming, owner Brett Lauderdale openly acknowledged the elephant in the room: Wuertz's history of expressing problematic views.

"In the past, Drake Wuertz shared some extreme views," Lauderdale wrote on social media. "He spread a rhetoric that was offensive to many and in cases, dangerous."

Rather than dismissing the criticism, Lauderdale posed a direct question to those questioning the booking: "What makes him worthy of a second chance and why does that second chance come in GCW of all places?"

The answer, Lauderdale explained, centers on a deeply personal debt. In March 2012, while struggling with opioid addiction during WrestleMania Week in Miami, Lauderdale found himself in crisis. Wuertz was the only person who extended help, taking him to his first Narcotics Anonymous meeting on March 29, 2012.

"Drake's compassion changed (saved) my life, he gave me a second chance," Lauderdale stated. "I've been waiting for years to return the favor and give him his second chance. In my heart, I feel like now is that time."

🔄 המשך Naraku Comes To Aid Of Vanity Project On WWE NXT, With Heatwave Implications

Jackson Drake will now pull double duty at WWE NXT Heatwave after a chaotic finish to his contender match against Shiloh Hill on Tuesday night's episode.

The match stipulations set high stakes for both competitors. A Drake victory would earn him an NXT North American Championship opportunity against Borne and grant his Vanity Project stablemates Brad Baylor and Ricky Smokes a shot at the NXT Tag Team Championships. Hill's path was simpler—a win would have granted him a singles match with Borne for the North American Title.

The closing moments saw Hill gain momentum with a pop-up forearm, but Myka Lockwood's distraction from the apron allowed Baylor and Smokes to pull Hill to the outside. When Smokes went for a punch, the referee intervened and ejected all three Vanity Project members from ringside.

Hill briefly fought back with a suicide dive to wipe out Baylor and Smokes before returning Drake to the ring. After a flying shoulder tackle, Hill climbed to the top rope for what appeared to be a finishing move. Drake knocked Hill off the turnbuckle, and in the ensuing chaos, the referee also took a bump.

That's when Naraku emerged from beneath the ring and sprayed green mist into Hill's face. Drake capitalized with a 450 Splash to secure the victory, setting up his title challenge alongside Baylor and Smokes' tag team opportunity at Heatwave.
