Bischoff believes WWE should wait another year before even considering the idea, emphasizing that the streak would need to grow naturally instead of arriving with flashing lights and an obvious sign telling fans they are watching the next Goldberg.

According to Bischoff, authenticity is everything in sports entertainment. The more authentic and organic it feels, the more the audience will suspend their disbelief, lean into what you are doing, and connect to the story and characters every week. That means WWE could not simply pick a powerhouse, start counting victories, and expect fans to fall in line. The streak would need to sneak up on the audience before people realized they were watching something special. Goldberg looked like an unstoppable killer, but behind the scenes, WCW knew he was still learning and built everything around the things he could already do well. Bill worked in his role because management realized how limited he was, which was not his fault since he was just new. You cannot pack ten years of experience in front of a live audience into twelve months in the Power Plant, meaning talent has to perform out in front of people to get real experience.

Bischoff noted they were smart enough to recognize Bill had certain limitations and built his story and matches around what he could do while minimizing risks.

Anyone trying to recreate that kind of run needs to stop getting cute and follow the simplest rule in wrestling. Keep it simple, stupid is about the best advice you could give somebody who is going to try to build that streak-type story. Keep it simple, keep it relatable, and do not overcomplicate it. Bischoff believes another undefeated monster will eventually come along, but he is not convinced WWE’s current booking style can easily keep someone protected in the same way.

WON Flashback: Is WWE SummerSlam 2002 the best SummerSlam ever?

August 25 2002 is the date of WWE SummerSlam 2002, which some argue is the greatest iteration of the summer pay-per-view event. The worst match on the card got two-and-a-quarter stars, the opener between Kurt Angle and Rey Mysterio is one of the best opening sprints in company history, and Shawn Michaels versus Triple H in the penultimate match of the night was also top tier. Reviewers evaluated the matches from the event with the following ratings: Kurt Angle versus Rey Mysterio received three and three-quarter stars, Ric Flair versus Chris Jericho received two and three-quarter stars, Edge versus Eddie Guerrero received three and one-quarter stars, Christian and Lance Storm defending the World Tag Team Titles against Booker T and Goldust received three stars, Rob Van Dam defeating Chris Benoit for the WWE Intercontinental Championship received three and a half stars, The Undertaker defeating Test received two and a quarter stars, Shawn Michaels defeating Triple H in an Unsanctioned Street Fight received four and a quarter stars, and Brock Lesnar defeating The Rock for the WWE Undisputed Title received three and a quarter stars.

Historical analysis of the event noted that it is almost hard to fathom that the WWE of most of that year is the same company of the past few weeks, though watching Raw the day after SummerSlam made it a bit easier to process. SummerSlam 2002 at the Nassau Coliseum went down as one of the best received pay-per-views in company history and the night of anointing Brock Lesnar as the company's new superstar. Of course, that decision was made months ago, and was rocky at first. Lesnar's early push was reminiscent of sputtering pushes given to others that some people in the company were behind, and others were railroading. Instead of being a monster, he was portrayed as a guy who needed manager interference to save him from being pinned by mid-carders like Bubba Ray Dudley and the Hardys. His win at King of the Ring came across far more impressive for the majority of fans who skipped that pay-per-view and only heard about it on television. But going back to old-fashioned booking, making a headliner, and long-term planning, paid off.

Preliminary indicators, including reports from theaters in Canada that carry the show, anecdotal sports bar response, and email volume of responses which have traditionally been a reliable accurate indicator of strong and weak buy rates, indicated far stronger pay-per-view numbers than anything since Wrestlemania. The reason, with all due respect to the return of Shawn Michaels, was because they made a main event that was hyped as something special. It didn't promise to be the four and a half star classic ahead of time, and that is not the lure that draws buy rates anyway, but it was presented as something special and was a product of super hype, the kind that all sports could learn from. While Lesnar winning the title was no secret, and the long-term plan was hardly a secret months ago, there were no changes in plan that would fool people, even though they might hurt the ultimate goal.

The end result seems to have been a successful show during a period when all aspects of business are way down. The idea was that to get Lesnar over as a fresh money draw, the guy he needed to beat was Rock, and that Rock would do it the right way. The fact he was untarnished, in that fans never saw him lose except for those who did at house shows before his TV push started, for all the talk of winning and losing not meaning anything, was part of the reason this worked. And they went full steam ahead in that direction, with the great finishing touches being the training videos for both that aired on many different shows. Rock, to his credit, put Lesnar over in the middle with the F-5, and whatever Lesnar's career does from here certainly can't be blamed on Rock not giving him the proper sendoff.

Sorgstock live wrestling event set for Bethel, New York

Woodstock, the 1969 music and arts festival that was held in Bethel, New York over the course of three days, defined the peak of the counterculture of the era. Promoters of the all-star music line-up expected maybe tens of thousands of fans, but when the event drew a few hundred thousand people, it became more than just a stacked concerted, it defined a generation. Jimi Hendrix, The Who, The Grateful Dead, and Janis Joplin were among the performers that saw their legacies etched in the history books because of their involvement at Woodstock.

In a microcosm of that scenario, a generation of Pittsburgh professional wrestling has been defined through the lens of the great, Mike Sorg, the premiere video producer of grappling content in the steel city.

Through the lineage of Bruno, Douglas, and Angle over the years, the region is undoubtedly a wrestling town. The fandom gets passed down through the generations similar to any other tradition. With those cornerstones as a foundation, it was almost natural that the area would have a storied and flourishing independent scene as well. It is not uncommon today for at least one, if not multiple pro wrestling events to be running every weekend somewhere around western Pennsylvania.

Unfortunately, much of that storied history, including when the Pittsburgh scene became a hotbed for sports entertainment in the late-90s as it rode the wave of momentum that saw the sport a part of pop culture during those years, was lost to time. Footage of Pittsburgh action from a few decades ago is sparse, as only a select number of television shows or live event VHS tapes were able to be saved for prosperity. Some presentations were captured on grainy VHS tapes before they were digitized to salvage them as somewhat of a grappling time capsule.
