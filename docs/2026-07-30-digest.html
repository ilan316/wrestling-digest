🔥 General Wrestling Commentary and Opinions

Al Snow has delivered a pointed critique of the wrestling industry's relationship with both media critics and fans, addressing what he sees as a fundamental hypocrisy in how wrestlers and companies handle external judgment.

Speaking on The Brand podcast with Jeff Lane, Snow argued that insecurity and the need for external validation have created a double standard throughout wrestling. He described how wrestlers enthusiastically embrace praise from Dave Meltzer and fans but object when those same voices criticize them. Snow explained that wrestlers celebrate when Meltzer gives them favorable coverage or five-star match ratings, calling him smart and praising his judgment. Yet when Meltzer or fans offer criticism, the same wrestlers reject that input as invalid.

Snow pointed out the contradiction in how wrestlers respond to fan opinions. When fans tell them "this is BS, you should be the champion, you're better than that, they're missing the boat with you," those wrestlers walk around the locker room repeating the sentiment as validation. However, Snow reminded them that fans are the paying customers whose ticket purchases and viewership enable their employment. He emphasized this point strongly: "Keep in mind, that fan bought a ticket or tuned in to see you do your job, not the other way around."

Despite his criticism, Snow stressed that he deeply appreciates wrestling fans and emphasized his gratitude repeatedly. He stated that he loves fans because without them he would have no job, and he cannot overstate how grateful and thankful he is for their support.

In related comments on The Brand, Snow expanded his criticism to address the relationship between wrestling companies and their audiences. He argued that WWE, AEW, and other wrestling companies do not owe fans explanations for their creative decisions. Snow accused wrestlers, administrators, and creative teams of inviting fan judgment while simultaneously complaining when audiences question their decisions.

Snow called the industry hypocritical for constantly revealing backstage information to wrestling journalists and online outlets, then resenting the scrutiny that naturally follows. He said it makes no sense for people within the business to be upset over judgment and questioning about backstage happenings when they themselves are continuously exposing that information and providing it to journalists to justify fan judgment.

Snow acknowledged that fans criticizing creative decisions, talent releases, and storyline choices do not possess complete information about why those decisions were made. However, he questioned why wrestling companies should owe the general public any explanation at all. He pointed out that AEW is not a publicly traded company, and to his knowledge, neither MLW nor NWA are. He noted that only WWE owes explanations to its stockholders, not to casual viewers sitting on their couches judging what the company does.

According to Snow, discontent is actually a valuable part of wrestling's storytelling process. He explained that the real art of wrestling involves making an audience want something, creating desire for a resolution, and building discontent to drive viewership and attendance to greater levels. This discontent serves the purpose of motivating fans to watch more so that companies can eventually satisfy that desire and capitalize on those emotions.

Snow argued that wrestling companies and wrestlers cannot completely pull back the curtain and expect fans not to judge what they learn. He framed it as a choice: either pull the curtain somewhat back to satisfy curiosity, or pull it all the way back and accept that fans will judge. Yet Snow observed that people in wrestling constantly try to have it both ways, which he views as impossible.

Snow articulated his vision of the fundamental role wrestlers play. He stated that the job of every wrestler is to be trusted by the audience because they paid their money. The wrestler's responsibility is to tell the audience what to think, feel, and believe, what to like and dislike. Through educating and directing the audience, wrestlers capitalize on those emotions to motivate continued viewership.

Snow used an extended metaphor comparing the wrestling company-audience relationship to a ship captain and passengers. He explained that wrestling companies are running the ship, and fans bought a ticket to go on the cruise. Passengers do not get to tell the captain to take a different route because they find the current one boring or enjoy a different view. Passengers are stuck on the boat until reaching the destination the captain takes them to.

However, Snow made a crucial point about those who work within the industry. He said that if you are on the boat as part of the crew or as one of the captains, you should not complain about the passengers telling you what to do. This contradiction exists because as the company drives the boat, it uses the PA system to tell passengers everything about what it is doing and why. Snow expressed exasperation at this fundamental inconsistency that pervades the wrestling business.

The broader context reveals ongoing tension in professional wrestling between those who create the product, those who perform it, and those who consume it. Wrestling's increased transparency about backstage operations and creative decision-making has created a situation where fans feel empowered to critique decisions, while many in the industry resent that scrutiny. Snow's perspective suggests that this conflict is largely self-inflicted, the result of deliberately pulling back the curtain while expecting to maintain control over how audiences respond to what they learn.

🔥 Chelsea Green Heart Surgery

Chelsea Green Opens Up About Emergency Heart Surgery and Her Triumphant Return to the Ring

WWE's Chelsea Green has bravely opened up about one of the scariest experiences of her life: emergency heart surgery and the trauma that followed. Speaking recently on TMZ's Inside The Ring, the SmackDown star revealed intimate details about her years-long battle with supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), the frightening medical emergency that finally led to surgery, and the emotional recovery journey that followed.

For nearly a decade, Green dealt with SVT, a condition that can suddenly cause the heart to beat extremely fast without warning. Despite her symptoms, doctors struggled for years to diagnose her condition because they could never record one of her episodes while she was connected to an EKG machine. That finally changed during WrestleMania week when everything came to a head at an unexpected moment.

Green recalled the pivotal moment that led to her diagnosis. She was at a signing at the WWE World store doing what should have been a routine autograph session with Fanatics representatives. She hadn't consumed any energy drinks or coffee—she was simply sitting there relaxing when everything changed suddenly. "I was sitting doing a signing. I hadn't had an energy drink; I hadn't been drinking coffee. I was just sitting there relaxing, hanging out with the Fanatics guys, and my heart rate jumped up to 228 beats per minute at a resting state," Green explained. "It stayed like that for well over 10 minutes, probably just under 15."

The unusually long duration of the episode proved to be a blessing in disguise. Because the tachycardia lasted so long, paramedics were able to arrive and perform an EKG while she was actively experiencing the condition. "Because it was so long, we were able to get the paramedics to come and do an EKG, and that is basically how we finally were able to catch it and book emergency SVT ablation surgery," Green said. Once doctors identified the condition, there was no time to waste. "When we finally caught it, I had to get surgery immediately. I think we caught it on a Saturday afternoon, and I was in surgery by Wednesday or Thursday."

The fear of what could have happened had driven Green for years. One of her biggest concerns was the possibility of suffering an SVT episode during a WWE match in front of thousands of fans. She couldn't predict when an attack might occur, and she had no way of knowing whether it would stop on its own once it started. "That was my fear, is that if it started in the ring, was it ever going to stop? I cannot be in the ring in front of 10 or 20,000 people and go into tachycardia and still finish a match. The risk of death is obviously huge," Green admitted. The threat of a potentially life-threatening episode happening during her profession was terrifying enough to make her seriously contemplate the dangers of continuing to perform.

However, the surgery itself turned out to be even more traumatic than Green had anticipated. She described the emotional weight of the experience in graphic detail. "When I finally was in surgery, or going into surgery, I think that's when everything really hit. The process of going to get that surgery, walking in through those doors, having it explained to me, being on the table, being woken up mid-surgery for them to send you into tachycardia to then prove that the surgery they did would help you, all of that was very, very traumatic," she said.

The most shocking part of the procedure was that Green had to wake up while doctors were still operating. This wasn't a mistake or accidental anesthesia complication—it was a necessary medical protocol. Doctors needed to intentionally trigger another SVT episode to confirm that the ablation surgery had successfully corrected the problem. Green described the sensations in harrowing detail: "For the last hour of the surgery or with some people maybe the last 20 minutes, they wake you up. They strap you down, they wake you up, you've got everything still going through your veins up into your heart, and they flush your IV with a medication that makes your heart speed up. So that exact feeling that I would get at the height of my SVT is what I felt immediately."

The experience left psychological scars that took months to heal. Green developed post-traumatic stress disorder from waking up during the procedure, experiencing recurring nightmares about the incident. "When I came out of it, I had, like, weird PTSD of being in that surgery and being woken up while still on the table and having them still operate," she revealed. "The waking up in surgery traumatized me more than I thought it would and more than I had expected it to. And so that was just something like I needed to talk through and mentally get over that because there's a lot of nightmares that I have about it."

The emotional toll was significant enough that Green sought professional help to process what she had experienced. She attended therapy sessions to work through the trauma and manage the psychological aftermath of the surgery. Despite the physical recovery going smoothly, the mental recovery required dedicated work and support.

Fortunately, several months after the procedure, Green's situation has improved dramatically. "Now we're a couple months out. I've spoken about it. I'm in the ring consistently every Friday night on Smackdown. I feel good. I feel better than I ever did before. I'm not getting the tachycardia attacks the way I used to. So it's a relief," she said. The surgery has finally given her the peace of mind she had been seeking for a decade, allowing her to perform without the constant fear of an unpredictable and potentially fatal episode.

Despite everything she endured—both the frightening health scare and the traumatic surgery experience—Green made one thing crystal clear: retirement was never an option. Her dedication to professional wrestling runs deeper than any fear or health concern. She reflected on her history of injuries throughout her career to illustrate her unwavering commitment to the business. "At this point, I've broken my nose five times. I broke my collarbone, I broke my ankle, I broke my arm three times. There's nothing that's going to stop me from wrestling unless I was dead," Green stated firmly.

Her passion for the industry, combined with her incredible resilience, has allowed her to bounce back from this frightening chapter. Green is currently scheduled to compete at WWE SummerSlam 2026 for the vacant Women's Championship, proving that she is ready to take on major opportunities and continue her career at the highest level.

Green's story serves as an inspiring reminder of the dangers professional wrestlers face, not only from the physicality of the sport but also from underlying health conditions that can strike without warning. Her willingness to open up about both the medical emergency and the psychological aftermath of her surgery demonstrates remarkable courage and vulnerability.

🔥 The Undertaker WWE Legacy and Vince McMahon

The Undertaker has recently opened up about his complex relationship with Vince McMahon during their three-decade partnership. On his Six Feet Under podcast, Taker revealed insights into two significant moments that defined their professional dynamic: one rare instance when McMahon acknowledged being wrong, and another major decision that remains unexplained to this day.

The Punjabi Prison match at WWE The Great American Bash 2006 stands out as the sole occasion when McMahon admitted he had made an incorrect creative call. The match was originally scheduled to feature The Undertaker versus The Great Khali, but when Khali became unavailable, Big Show was inserted into the match instead. During the planning stages, Undertaker proposed an innovative finish involving jumping from the inner cage to the outer cage to escape, believing this would create an unforgettable visual moment that would surprise the audience. McMahon, however, wanted the wrestlers to fight through the gate before escaping the structure.

Undertaker attempted to convince McMahon that his version would generate a superior reaction from the crowd. He recalled the creative disagreement with specificity: "I had the idea that I wanted to jump from the inner cage to the outer cage and get out that way. I thought, 'Man, that would be such a cool visual. They won't expect it. It'll look amazing.' And we went at it, and back and forth. It finally got to the point where, 'I don't wanna hear anything else about it. This is the way we're gonna do it.' I'm like, 'Fine. It's gonna suck. When it sucks, it's gonna be on you,' which he didn't appreciate that either."

When the match aired, McMahon's vision failed to deliver the desired impact. The gate escape spot fell completely flat with the audience. Undertaker didn't need to say anything to his boss backstage; McMahon already understood what had transpired. The moment between them was remarkably understated yet significant. Undertaker described the aftermath: "We did it the way he wanted it and it was flat. I mean, it was like womp, womp, womp, womp. And so I go back and I just looked at him. I didn't have to say a word. He knew. And he goes, almost under his breath, he goes, 'Uh, yeah, I think you were right on that cage spot.' And I was like, 'Really?' In stark contrast to that rare moment of acknowledgment, McMahon's decision regarding a potential Undertaker versus Sting dream match at WrestleMania 31 remains shrouded in mystery. For years, wrestling fans had clamored for these two iconic figures, representing WWE and WCW respectively, to finally face each other on wrestling's grandest stage. When Sting debuted in WWE in late 2014, WrestleMania 31 seemed like the perfect opportunity to finally deliver this long-awaited encounter. Instead, McMahon booked Undertaker against Bray Wyatt while assigning Sting to face Triple H, which ultimately became Sting's only WrestleMania appearance.

The decision puzzles both Undertaker and the broader wrestling community. Undertaker has never received a clear explanation from McMahon regarding why the match never came to fruition. Speaking about it on his podcast Q&A session, Undertaker expressed his bewilderment: "Obviously, the people had already been clamoring for that match for a long time, and it seems like a no-brainer. Like, why on earth would you not do that? But for whatever reason, Vince did not see it, didn't like it, didn't want it. And I was like, man, this is a home run. This is an easy main event for a pay-per-view."

What makes McMahon's stance even more puzzling is the lack of substantive discussion about it. Undertaker revealed that he and McMahon never had an in-depth conversation exploring the reasoning behind the decision. "But I don't really have an answer. I never really had an in-depth conversation with Vince about why he didn't want to do it. My goodness, a Sting vs. Undertaker? I mean, again, he obviously knows more about business than I do, but that one just seems like a home run that was just — they decided to walk the guy instead of pitch to him? I don't know. I don't know what happened there. I mean, I don't have any regrets. It wasn't my decision. But, my goodness, I would have loved to have been able to have that match. I guess it just wasn't meant to be."

Interestingly, Sting shared Undertaker's confusion about the decision. The two wrestlers ran into each other around the time of WrestleMania 31 and discovered they were on the same plane together. Undertaker has come to regard this missed opportunity as arguably the biggest missed moment in WrestleMania history. "Yeah, I think Sting, that's probably got to be the biggest miss of a WrestleMania. Those two characters, that would have done some incredible business."

Both performers have since moved on to different phases of their careers. Undertaker now works as a key voice behind the scenes in booking Lucha Libre AAA, while Sting maintains occasional AEW appearances and convention work. His sons Steven and Garrett are now breaking into the wrestling business themselves.

The contrast between these two stories illustrates the complexity of the Undertaker-McMahon relationship. The Punjabi Prison moment serves as a rare exception that proves even the most powerful figures in wrestling can occasionally recognize when they were wrong, yet the Sting decision demonstrates that such acknowledgment remains the exception rather than the rule. For wrestling fans, the Undertaker versus Sting match will forever remain one of the industry's greatest "what if" scenarios, a dream match that logic dictates should have happened but never did.
