Tatha-Nanandji, who promoted WCWA Wrestling in Arkansas, had accused WWE of copying a recurring visual sequence linked to his Tier 1 faction. The sequence showed group members coming together and raising one finger in the air. Tatha-Nanandji claimed WWE had access to footage showing the Tier 1 sequence after a wrestler who had worked for WCWA requested footage that could potentially be used for scouting. He also claimed that an unnamed WWE employee attended a WCWA event in March 2019 and stayed in contact with him through 2021, and that the Tier 1 sequence then appeared on WWE television weeks after the footage became available.

However, the court found that Tatha-Nanandji failed to show that anyone at WWE had actually seen the copyrighted material containing the sequence. The judge also found issues with the alleged similarities between the two presentations. While Tatha-Nanandji had copyrights covering the entrance, the court ruled that there was no copying of the original elements of the work.

The ruling pointed out inconsistencies in how the Tier 1 sequence appeared across the plaintiffs exhibits. The court noted that the Tier 1 sequence does not appear to be consistent across the Plaintiffs exhibits as alleged in the Complaint. Defendants alleged infringing material is consistent in each iteration of the exhibits provided, and the court finds that a reasonable person, reviewing the materials together, would only see the similarities amongst the Defendants clips but not the Plaintiffs clips.

The copyright claim required Tatha-Nanandji to establish both access and substantial similarity. The court concluded that the plaintiff failed to meet the requirements of access and substantial similarity as required for copyright infringement, and as such, failed to state a claim for direct copyright infringement.

WWE and 2K had argued that one wrestling promotion could not control a basic gesture. The number 1 gesture and raising an award or prize are universal symbols used throughout the world to tout one’s accomplishments. If Plaintiff is permitted to monopolize such commonplace gestures and poses, it would put at risk athletes, sports fans and any other group of people who raise their fingers or awards in victory.

WWE also argued that the actual presentations were different in several ways, pointing to factors such as timing, group positioning, camera angles, and lighting. No court has ever permitted a copyright infringement claim to proceed without substantial similarity of protectable expression, which is completely lacking here. The court ultimately agreed that the plaintiff had not established the necessary elements to keep the case alive, preventing Tatha-Nanandji from re-filing the same claim before the court.

Editorial on the cultural significance of lucha libre masks in Mexico

Mexico’s legendary Lucha Libre masks are more than just an accessory

There are few symbols in professional wrestling as instantly recognizable as the colorful masks of Mexico’s lucha libre. When WWE initially bought AAA in 2025, I was pretty ignorant of the wrestling culture and style, except for an infatuation with the now-defunct Lucha Underground. Having followed CMLL via my ROH recaps and then jumping into the world of AAA when their Fox Mexico show became a reality, I have absolutely fallen in love with it. It has rekindled many of the feelings of excitement and joy that I had watching wrestling when I was younger in WWE’s arguable heyday. As I learn more about the culture and history of Lucha Libre, Mexico and its people, I can’t help but become even more fascinated and one of the most interesting parts of the presentation, that being the mask which dominates much of my thinking about stuff time. I’ve found it difficult to find official histories, books and films about Lucha Libre, but with a little digging, and a little help from Google Translate, I have managed to pull together some information and history of Lucha Libre masks and their significance to the culture in and out of the ring. And yes, I was wearing a mask the whole time I wrote this.

The beginnings of Lucha Libre

The elaborate masks of Lucha Libre aren’t simply costumes—they are pieces of identity, mythology and tradition that have become inseparable from Mexican wrestling culture. The tradition of masked wrestling in Mexico dates back to 1933, when Salvador Lutteroth founded the Empresa Mexicana de Lucha Libre. Looking for ways to capture the imagination of Mexican audiences, American wrestler Corbin James Massey wrestled in Mexico unmasked in 1933 as Cyclone Mackey before debuting the masked La Maravilla Enmascarada character in 1934, wearing a crude leather mask made by local shoemaker Antonio Martínez. According to the promotion's history, that early experiment quickly evolved into something much bigger. Over time, mask-making became a specialized art form and an essential part of what makes lucha libre unique.

More than just a wrestling accessory

A lucha mask isn’t simply flashy athletic equipment. Creating a lucha libre mask is a highly specialized craft with techniques passed down through generations of artisans. Materials including lycra, patent leather and vinyl are chosen for their flexibility and breathability, helping masks stay securely in place while wrestlers perform high-flying moves. It transforms the wrestler underneath into a larger-than-life character with designs often drawing on pre-Columbian imagery and warrior traditions. Aztec eagles, jaguars, Mayan deities and supernatural creatures are among the motifs that can appear on a mask. The mask allows ordinary people to take on heroic archetypes deeply connected to Mexican folklore.

The mask’s commitment to character and anonymity is closely connected to kayfabe, the professional wrestling tradition of maintaining the illusion that the characters and stories are real. For masked luchadores, that can mean remaining in character long after they leave the ring. Masked wrestlers may attend press conferences, travel through airports and appear on television while still wearing their masks. Maintaining that secrecy helps keep the mythical character separate from everyday reality.

Good guys, bad guys and the ultimate bet

The masks also play an important role in lucha libre storytelling. Wrestlers generally fall into two broad camps: the heroic técnicos and the villainous rudos. A masked técnico represents virtue, sportsmanship and colorful justice, while a rudo embraces rule-breaking, chaos and defiance. The mask helps make this classic struggle between good and evil immediately visible to the audience. That symbolism of the mask becomes even more powerful during a Lucha de Apuestas, or wager match. In one of lucha libre’s most dramatic traditions, two wrestlers can put their most prized possessions on the line in a mask versus mask match.
