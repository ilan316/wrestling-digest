🔥 Thunder Rosa Departs Busted Open Radio Amid WWE Radio Launch

Thunder Rosa's run on Busted Open Radio has come to an end following WWE's takeover of SiriusXM's wrestling programming. The AEW star confirmed her departure on Friday through a statement posted on social media, officially ending weeks of speculation about her status after the announcement of WWE Radio's launch.

Rosa had been a regular contributor to Busted Open for four years, serving as a rotating co-host on the show. However, her appearances had become increasingly infrequent in recent months, with her last call-in coming earlier in July during a special episode celebrating host Dave LaGreca's birthday. When WWE and SiriusXM announced the rebranding of Pro Wrestling Nation 24/7 to WWE Radio, Rosa was notably absent from all press releases and lineup announcements.

In her statement, Rosa reflected on her time with the show, saying "After four unforgettable years, it's time to turn the page. Some chapters in life change you forever, and this has been one of them." She emphasized that being part of Busted Open was about far more than just sitting behind a microphone, crediting the platform with giving her the opportunity to share her perspective, connect with wrestling fans worldwide, and celebrate the sport she loves.

The AEW Women's World Champion expressed particular gratitude to Tony Khan for believing in her and trusting her to represent AEW on one of the most respected wrestling shows in the industry. She also extended thanks to the show's hosts and production team, including Dave LaGreca, Tommy Dreamer, Bully Ray, and Mark Henry, saying they made her feel like family and challenged her to improve as a broadcaster.

Rosa made clear that her departure does not mark the end of her media presence, stating "The microphone may be off for now, but my voice isn't going anywhere. This isn't goodbye. It's simply the beginning of a new chapter." She promised to continue sharing her passion for professional wrestling with audiences in whatever capacity comes next.

The departure comes as WWE took control of SiriusXM's wrestling channel in a major partnership announced on July 24, 2026, with WWE Radio officially launching on July 27. The new station features the core cast of Busted Open including Dave LaGreca, Tommy Dreamer, Nic Nemeth, Bully Ray, and Mark Henry, along with other shows like "Cheap Heat Live" and "Notsam Wrestling," plus a slate of WWE-exclusive podcasts and live commentary for all WWE premium live events.

Industry observers had speculated that Rosa's departure was inevitable given the nature of the partnership. As reported by Dave Meltzer and the Wrestling Observer, those close to the situation believed it would be impossible to keep an AEW talent on a WWE-controlled show. Fightful Select also confirmed that Rosa was not expected to continue on Busted Open and reported that the show would not be covering AEW going forward.

When questioned by callers about whether AEW coverage would continue on Busted Open under WWE Radio, hosts Dave LaGreca and Bully Ray managed to avoid mentioning AEW in any capacity while maintaining that nothing would change about the show's tone or approach. LaGreca had vowed that WWE wants Busted Open to remain as it has always been, claiming the company does not want to change their critical and balanced approach to professional wrestling coverage. Bully Ray suggested the show would cover more than just WWE, though he did not specifically address whether that would include AEW programming.

Rosa's exit represents perhaps the clearest indication yet that the new WWE-SiriusXM partnership will fundamentally alter the landscape of independent wrestling coverage on the platform. Her four-year tenure on Busted Open allowed her to represent AEW's perspective on one of the industry's most prominent audio programs, and her departure signals a significant shift in how SiriusXM's wrestling content will be positioned moving forward.

🔥 AEW Star Kyle Fletcher Comments and Interviews

Kyle Fletcher has quickly become one of AEW's most compelling young stars since entering the promotion in 2022. The 27-year-old Australian wrestler arrived as one half of a midcard tag team but has since evolved into a decorated singles champion and a key figure in the promotion's top heel faction. Now, as the current International Champion preparing to defend his title against Bandido at AEW Redemption in Montreal, Fletcher is reflecting on his remarkable journey and the various aspects of his personal and professional life.

One of the most impactful stories from Fletcher's early career involves a comment from WWE Chairman Vince McMahon that nearly derailed his wrestling dreams. During a recent interview, Fletcher revealed how he remembered watching an old WWE interview where McMahon was asked if he thought an Australian wrestler would ever become a regular on WWE television. According to Fletcher, McMahon's response was blunt and discouraging. "I hate that accent," the WWE legend said. For a young eight-year-old Fletcher watching at home in Australia, this moment was deeply demoralizing. "And I was like, 'Oh, awesome. Yeah, great. Cool.' That feels really good as an eight-year-old to hear," Fletcher recalled with evident sarcasm. However, Fletcher's story demonstrates just how much the wrestling industry has changed. When he was growing up, there were almost no Australian wrestlers in major North American promotions. Today, the landscape is dramatically different, with WWE featuring stars like Rhea Ripley, Grayson Waller, and Bronson Reed, while AEW showcases Toni Storm and Kyle Fletcher himself. All of these wrestlers have found significant success while maintaining their authentic Australian accents, proving McMahon's outdated perspective completely wrong.

Despite his current success as a champion, Fletcher insists he is far from finished evolving as a performer. In discussing his character development, he emphasized his commitment to constant growth. "I'm not a finished product, and I don't think I ever want to be a finished product. I want to constantly be evolving with the business," he explained. Fletcher's transformation has been intentional and gradual rather than a sudden reinvention. When he began his heel run in AEW, he shaved his head as a symbolic fresh start. "It was something that wasn't necessarily done overnight. I didn't come in on that day with like a fully fleshed out character, and I didn't go, 'This is who I am. this is who the new Kyle Fletcher is.' I knew what the starting point was. I knew that I wanted to get rid of my hair. I wanted to have like a clean slate. I wanted to start from scratch," he said.

From that initial change, Fletcher built his character piece by piece through trial and error. He experimented with new gear, different ways of speaking, and various looks, paying careful attention to what resonated with the live audience. "I think one of the things I love about pro wrestling is it's constantly evolving and constantly changing. And I think as a character, you also have to do that. I don't think you can come in and be married to 'this is what I am, this is who I am, I'm sticking with this no matter what,' because, you know, might not work," Fletcher explained. He continued, "So when I came in, I did the head shave, it really did feel like a clean slate. So after that, from that point onwards, I was trying stuff out. I was trying new gear. I was trying new ways of speaking, new whatever, whatever look it was. So I was trying things out and it took a while and some things didn't stick and some things did. And then I think over time you kind of start, 'okay, that worked. I'll keep that. Okay, that worked. I'll keep that. Okay, here's this other idea. Let me try and implement that. Oh my god, everyone loved that.' And I think it is still evolving and still changing, which I think is the beautiful part of it."

Off screen, Fletcher maintains a strong relationship with fellow AEW star Skye Blue, whom he has been with for nearly four years. When asked what makes their relationship work in an industry where many wrestling couples have struggled, Fletcher credited their fundamental compatibility. "There's probably a lot of different things. I think the main one is we are the same person in so many different ways. We have our own experiences, and we've come up through the wrestling business in different ways, but at the heart of it, we are the same person. We're both so goofy and silly and love having fun all the time. But then also we're both very anxious, and we like to overthink everything, and especially within wrestling," he said. Fletcher emphasized the unique advantage of having a partner who understands both the wrestling business and the personal struggles that come with it. "It's so nice to have somebody there that not only understands the wrestling business, but understands you, and what you're going through and the feelings you're going through. She's always there for me exactly when I need it and vice versa. We're exactly what each other needs at any given point, which I think is awesome. We both love wrestling so much. WWE and AEW Comparison Essays and Opinion Pieces

CORPORATIONS AND LEADERSHIP: THE ONGOING BATTLE FOR WRESTLING'S SOUL

The professional wrestling industry continues to grapple with a fundamental question that has plagued it for decades: can major promotions maintain creative excellence while operating under corporate oversight? Two recent opinion pieces tackle this issue from different angles, examining how WWE and AEW have handled their respective corporate structures and leadership philosophies.

The argument against corporate involvement in wrestling has historical precedent. Throughout wrestling history, promotions that have fallen under increasing corporate control have typically seen a decline in creative quality and fan satisfaction. This pattern appears across multiple organizations, from WCW to ECW to TNA to WWE itself at various points in its existence. The common thread is that when corporations become involved in day-to-day creative decisions, the authenticity and organic storytelling that professional wrestling fans crave tends to disappear.

WWE's current situation serves as a cautionary tale. Following Vince McMahon's departure in June 2022, Triple H took over creative operations and immediately restored fan confidence with compelling storylines and talented performers who regained their equity in the eyes of the audience. The Bloodline received exceptional creative direction, main event stars like Roman Reigns, CM Punk, Seth Rollins, and Gunther were presented as must-see talent, and the overall product felt rejuvenated. However, this renaissance proved short-lived.

The formation of TKO Corporation as a holding company by Endeavor to oversee both UFC and WWE marked a turning point. When The Rock was placed on the TKO board of directors, creative control began shifting away from Triple H's vision toward corporate interests. Endeavor's involvement became increasingly problematic because the company represents numerous WWE wrestlers as talent agents, creating obvious conflicts of interest. More troublingly, TKO immediately began implementing aggressive price increases across all WWE offerings, demonstrating that financial extraction took priority over maintaining fan goodwill.

The financial consequences have been dramatic and undeniable. SummerSlam ticket prices that originally sold for seventy-six dollars in nose-bleed sections were reduced to twenty-five dollars, representing a massive sixty-seven percent discount. This steep markdown is not a clever promotional tactic but rather a public admission of failure on multiple fronts. It acknowledges that WWE engaged in absurd price gouging that alienated fans, and it demonstrates that creative mismanagement under TKO oversight has significantly diminished the drawing power of top stars.

The current WWE roster, once packed with superstars fans were eager to see, now appears worn out and fatigued. The performers themselves seem exhausted from working in front of audiences who have similarly lost enthusiasm due to financial strain. Fans supporting an overpriced product that no longer delivers compelling storytelling have gradually reduced their discretionary spending on wrestling. In just two years, corporate interference transformed WWE from an impressive money-generating machine into a struggling enterprise requiring steep discounts just to fill venues.

History suggests this outcome was entirely predictable. Vince McMahon's later years as WWE chairman, particularly after returning to the USA Network during the late 2000s, demonstrated how corporate partnerships influence creative decisions. The PG era, promoted squeaky-clean babyfaces the audience didn't want to support, and unnecessary censorship all flowed from corporate relationships and sponsor demands. When toy companies and other corporate partners gained influence over creative direction, wrestling suffered. Even the removal of Mandy Rose from WWE television was dictated by corporate toy company executives rather than determined by wrestling logic.

The TNA documentary produced by Vice's Dark Side of the Ring exposed a similar corporate problem, though in a different form. TNA's ownership by Panda Energy through Dixie Carter created a situation where corporate interests consistently overrode wrestling-specific decision-making. While Dixie Carter received harsh criticism in the documentary, much of the blame ultimately extends to corporate influence and how it shaped her decision-making as president. The documentary revealed that Jeff Jarrett was sent home in July 2009 after Dixie Carter perceived a personal betrayal regarding his dating of Karen Angle without informing her first. However, this incident demonstrates how corporate-influenced leadership often makes personal considerations and office politics central to business decisions rather than focusing on what serves the wrestling product best.

The political maneuvering that the TNA documentary exposed, particularly regarding Vince Russo's role in the company, illustrates another danger of corporate structures in wrestling. Russo's documented pattern of privately badmouthing other creative team members to management while publicly feigning innocence exemplifies the workplace dysfunction that corporate environments encourage. Corporate structures create hierarchies, office politics, and power plays that distance decision-makers from the actual creative work and fan interests.

AEW presents an interesting counterpoint to this corporate control discussion. The promotion operates under Tony Khan's personal vision and leadership without the same corporate interference that WWE experiences. However, this autonomy has also created its own set of problems, though they manifest differently. Khan has positioned himself as a fan-first owner who supposedly values audience input and suggestions, yet in reality, he operates his promotion according to his personal preferences with little genuine regard for outside opinions.

Khan's AEW operates under a philosophy that can be summarized as "my way or the highway." While the promotion's owner occasionally nods and smiles at fan criticisms and suggestions, he consistently disregards them if they conflict with his vision. Fans who object to pay-per-view events that extend well past midnight on the East Coast, even on Sunday nights when many must work Monday morning, are simply told that their preferences don't matter. Rather than accommodating reasonable fan feedback about scheduling, Khan doubles down on his preferences, even joking about the possibility of running twelve-hour PPV events that would test fan commitment to absurd degrees.

Complaints about Dynamite's runtime receiving excessive overruns with additional programming fail to influence scheduling decisions. When fans suggest that women's wrestlers deserve better positioning and more substantial television time, Khan's solution is to give them additional segments positioned in overruns and commercial breaks, which paradoxically devalues their matches by presenting them as secondary content. Viewers who hope for clear card announcements before attending shows are rebuffed with the response that they should simply show up and discover the card in real time. Fans attending combo Dynamite and Collision tapings who question what wrestlers will perform on which show are explicitly told not to ask.

The roster situation at AEW reflects Khan's unchecked control. Rather than carefully building and developing a focused group of talented performers, Khan continuously brings in new wrestlers, tag teams, and former WWE talent who visit Jacksonville periodically for extended taping sessions. The promotion maintains a bloated roster where numerous performers collect checks while rarely appearing on television. Wrestlers like Britt Baker and others who aren't currently featured simply vanish from programming with no explanation or timeline for return, while continuing to be paid.

While WWE suffers from excessive corporate interference that stifles creative autonomy, AEW struggles with the opposite problem: complete creative autonomy wielded by an owner who appears to view the promotion as a personal plaything rather than an entertainment product designed to serve an audience. Khan's approach dismisses fan input not because corporate interests override it, but because Khan has decided his vision supersedes audience preferences entirely.

The wrestling industry faces a genuine dilemma without easy solutions. Corporate involvement in wrestling promotions typically produces decline through interference and profit-focused decision-making that undermines creative integrity. Yet unchecked personal control by an owner with little accountability to the audience can prove equally problematic, resulting in self-indulgent booking and business practices that frustrate the fanbase. The ideal scenario involves a creative visionary with genuine wrestling knowledge operating with sufficient autonomy to execute their vision while remaining appropriately responsive to audience interests and feedback.

WWE's golden period under Triple H's 2022-2023 tenure demonstrated what's possible when a wrestling professional with proven creative instincts receives the freedom to operate. That period was cut short by corporate pressure and financial greed. AEW's struggles indicate that even well-intentioned fan ownership can become problematic when the owner conflates personal control with creative excellence and dismisses audience feedback as irrelevant.

The wrestling industry requires a balance that both WWE and AEW have failed to achieve. Promotions need sufficient corporate resources and business infrastructure to support the product, but not corporate interference in creative decisions made by executives with no wrestling knowledge or experience. Simultaneously, promotions require leadership that respects and considers audience interests while maintaining a clear creative vision. Finding this balance remains wrestling's greatest ongoing challenge.

According to the filing submitted pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 41(a)(1)(A)(ii), the Plaintiff Janel Grant, by and through her undersigned counsel, filed the voluntary dismissal of the action. The filing states: "Plaintiff, Defendant World Wrestling Entertainment, LLC, and Defendant Vincent K. McMahon (together, the 'Parties') stipulate and agree that this action and all claims pled herein are hereby dismissed with prejudice, but without prejudice as to the Parties' rights to pursue their claims and defenses in arbitration in accordance with their agreement to arbitrate."

Once the court approves this voluntary dismissal, the case will officially move into private arbitration, taking the dispute out of the public court system. The arbitration process will provide a resolution to the case. If a settlement is reached through arbitration, the terms of that agreement could remain confidential, marking a significant shift from the public proceedings that have defined this case.

Michael Cole Narrowly Avoided a 2,000-Foot Fall During Rock-Climbing Accident

Michael Cole has called many scary moments in WWE over the years, but one of the scariest moments of his life happened outside the ring. Cole explained that reaching the top of the 13,950-foot mountain wasn't the hardest part. After making it to the summit and even FaceTiming his wife, his guide reminded everyone that getting back down would be even more dangerous. The accident happened while the group was climbing back down. Cole had just rappelled about 85 feet and safely reached the next ledge. He then tried to unclip the rope from his harness so the climbers above could use it. After struggling for a while, he finally pulled the rope free. But the sudden movement caused his boot to slip on an icy rock.

Everything happened in an instant. Cole said he rolled down the mountain for about 55 to 60 feet before somehow stopping just a few inches from a fatal drop. Cole recounted the experience in detail: "I went off first, came down the mountain, landed, and I was having trouble getting the rope unclipped from my harness so I could send it up to the guy so Frenchie could come down next. As I slipped, all I remember was tumbling head over heels for about 55 to 60 feet and legitimately stopping from here to where they're standing from going off a 2,000-foot drop."

Cole thought he had broken his ankle during the fall. As he sat there trying to catch his breath, his first thoughts were about his wife and how close he had come to dying. After the fall, Cole told his guide he believed his ankle was broken. The guide then explained the options Cole faced in that moment.

Cole explained what happened next: "And I had a helmet on, thank God, because it protected me from any concussion, but I thought I broke my ankle. 🔥 Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery Merger Legal Updates

The merger between Paramount Skydance and Warner Bros. Discovery, valued at $111 billion, is officially on hold pending the outcome of an antitrust legal challenge. On Friday, Paramount agreed with a coalition of state attorneys to postpone the sale, entering into a joint stipulation that prevents the companies from closing the transaction until five days after the antitrust trial concludes or June 1, 2027, whichever occurs sooner. A trial date has not yet been set.

The legal battle began when 12 state attorneys general, led by California Attorney General Rob Bonta, filed a lawsuit arguing that the proposed merger would violate antitrust laws and create an anticompetitive landscape, particularly within the theatrical exhibition industry. The lawsuit is based on a more than century-old antitrust statute. A federal judge granted a temporary restraining order early in the week that initially prevented the sale from closing for 28 days. U.S. District Judge Araceli Martinez-Olguin subsequently extended this restraining order through August 18 to allow both sides time to come to an agreement on the schedule and format for legal proceedings on a preliminary injunction. Arguments on the preliminary injunction motion were originally scheduled for August 3, though that hearing was canceled following Friday's agreement.

Paramount characterized the agreement as a "significant win" in a statement, emphasizing that it provides "a direct path to a trial based on the evidence" and represents "the fastest and clearest way to prove that this transaction is good for competition, good for consumers, and good for creators." The company noted that dozens of competition authorities around the world have already reached this conclusion. Paramount also dismissed the plaintiffs' market definitions, saying they "bear no relationship to the realities of today's marketplace and cannot withstand scrutiny."

However, the delay comes at a substantial financial cost to Paramount. The company had hoped to complete the merger by fall, but the extended timeline will trigger significant additional expenses. If the deal does not close by September 30, Warner Bros. Discovery shareholders will receive a "ticking fee" of $7 million per day, totaling approximately $650 million per quarter. If the merger is delayed until June 2027, as the agreement allows, this will add approximately $1.7 billion to the total deal cost. Should the merger fail to close due to regulatory issues, Paramount is required to pay Warner Bros. Discovery a breakup fee of $7 billion.

The situation parallels another large media merger, between Nexstar Media and Tegna, which is similarly on pause with a trial scheduled for mid-2027. Rob Bonta has filed the lawsuit in that case as well.

The merger has significant implications for professional wrestling, specifically for AEW. Warner Bros. Discovery currently holds the domestic broadcast rights to AEW programming through 2027, with an option to extend for an additional year. AEW owner Tony Khan has been supportive of the merger, stating he was "thrilled" about the potential acquisition and believes it would be "great for AEW." One of the key strategic benefits for AEW involves the planned merger of HBO Max and Paramount Plus streaming services. Paramount CEO David Ellison has stated that combining these services would bring the merged companies over 200 million direct-to-consumer subscribers globally, potentially providing significantly more exposure for AEW's programming. However, if the antitrust trial extends beyond 2027, the resolution could create uncertainty for AEW's media landscape and broadcast arrangements during a critical period for the company's contractual negotiations and future distribution strategy.

Wrestling Title Changes and Historical Records

July 24 has been another pretty busy day for title changes throughout wrestling history. According to wrestling records, there have been 271 major and notable title changes on this date historically, making it one of the most significant dates on the professional wrestling calendar.

The earliest recorded July 24th title change dates back to 1957, when Lou Thesz won the NWA World Heavyweight Title. This marked the beginning of a long tradition of significant championship transitions on this date. The momentum continued in 1967 when Bruno Sammartino and Spiros Arion won the WWWF United States Tag Team Titles, solidifying July 24th as a date of historical importance in wrestling.

Throughout the 1980s and early 1990s, the date continued to produce notable champions. Sensational Sherri won the WWF Women's Title in 1987, bringing prestige to women's wrestling on this date. In 1991, Mitsuharu Misawa and Toshiaki Kawada captured the AJPW World Tag Team Titles, and Blue Panther won the Mexican National Middleweight Title in 1992.

The year 1996 proved to be particularly active, with three major title changes occurring on July 24th. Rick Steiner and Scott Steiner won the WCW World Tag Team Titles, Tsuyoshi Kikuchi claimed the AJPW World Junior Heavyweight Title, and Kenta Kobashi captured the AJPW Unified Triple Crown Heavyweight Title, demonstrating the date's significance across multiple wrestling promotions.

The late 1990s and early 2000s continued the tradition with several important champions. Edge won the WWF Intercontinental Title in 1999, followed by Lance Storm capturing the WCW Hardcore Title in 2000. The year 2001 saw two title changes on July 24th, with Kanyon winning the WCW United States Title and Kurt Angle winning the WCW World Heavyweight Title. Genki Horiguchi added to the record by winning the NWA World Welterweight Title in 2002.

In more recent years, July 24th has continued to produce championship transitions. Heidenreich and Road Warrior Animal won the WWE Tag Team Titles in 2005, followed by Atsushi Aoki and Hikaru Sato winning the All Asia Tag Team Titles in 2016. The most recent entry in this historical record came in 2022, when Kyle Fletcher and Mark Davis won the NJPW Strong Openweight Tag Team Titles, adding another chapter to July 24th's legacy as a significant date for professional wrestling title changes.
