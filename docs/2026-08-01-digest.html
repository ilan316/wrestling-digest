Rebel Heart is an episode of Wednesday Night Dynamite coming up in Athens, Georgia on September 9th. Will Ospreay AEW Involvement

Will Ospreay is heading into the biggest match of his life on August 30 when he challenges Kenny Omega for the AEW World Championship in the main event of AEW All In London at Wembley Stadium. This will mark the third time AEW has held an event at the biggest football stadium in England since 2023, something that sounded inconceivable to many wrestling fans not that long ago. However, Ospreay is hoping both he and AEW can get some jaded fans back into the business.

During a recent interview, Ospreay was asked about the casual wrestling audience, or even those who haven't watched since the famed WWE Attitude Era who aren't as in tune with things these days, and what he would say in order to get them to buy a ticket to All In London. In the simplest possible terms, Ospreay explained that wrestling is for everyone these days, and that it has evolved so much since the late 1990s.

"If you've not watched since the Attitude Era days, we have far surpassed those days with just how our athleticism and our in-ring action take front and centre spotlight," Ospreay told the original source. "It's so important now to present wrestling in a sports environment, for myself anyway, to show people that there are different ways of telling different stories in wrestling. There are so many people now from every walk of life that get to come through as a fan and celebrate all together. It reaches out to different cultures, different races, different religions. It's something that bonds us all, and the fact that it's all going to culminate in Wembley Stadium is such a momentous thing."

At the time of writing, over 35,000 tickets have been sold for this year's All In event with one month to go until bell time, and AEW is hoping to top the 53,000 tickets sold for the previous Wembley event, All In London 2024.

Between major wrestling promotions running stadium shows across the globe, it can feel like wrestling is in a very good place right now. However, professional wrestling is still somewhat of a niche interest in the eyes of the casual viewer, and when talking about introducing new fans to AEW, Ospreay understands that wrestling isn't for everyone. With that said, the only way he can sell All In London to someone who isn't familiar with wrestling is simply by telling his story.

"If you're not interested, I can't convince you otherwise," Ospreay explained, via Wrestling Inc. H/T. "The only thing that I can tell you is this is something that I've wanted to do since I was a 14-year-old boy, and I could never imagine that this was the dream in front of me. I couldn't tell you that this was it, because it was never done before. There was wrestling once upon a time in Wembley Stadium. It was in 1992, and then that was it. AEW has come along and has rewritten the book of what professional wrestling is, and if you want to give it a chance, or even just give me a chance, I think I might be able to change your mind."

When asked what match he would show someone in order to get his message across, Ospreay pointed to a bout he has previously called one of the proudest moments of his entire career. "That matchup at Dynasty against Bryan Danielson in 2024 was something where I felt like I finally became the guy that I wanted to become, and to be in there with a legend that is Bryan, I think is a perfect mix between the technical ground style of professional wrestling and the high-paced hybrid style that I was able to bring into wrestling," he said.

AEW Dynamite Viewership Report

When analyzed against recent trends, this week's numbers proved nearly identical to the 10-week average. The program averaged a 0.12 demo rating and 667,700 total viewers over the previous 10 weeks, with this week's 676,000 viewers falling essentially in line with that benchmark. The demo rating matched the average exactly, indicating consistency in the show's performance despite the slight total viewership decrease.

AEW Fan Criticism Commentary

A wrestling news editor has voiced significant concerns about the increasing time commitment required to cover AEW programming, describing the experience as increasingly exhausting despite acknowledging the promotion's strong wrestling product.

The editor's frustrations came to a head following AEW Dynamite's recent 30-minute overrun, which set a record for the program. This extended broadcast followed an earlier PPV event that was pushed an hour earlier in the day but ended up running for an additional hour, concluding just minutes before 9 pm PT. The editor had initially hoped the early start would allow for quicker coverage completion, but instead found themselves dedicating even more time to the show.

While the editor acknowledges that their situation as a professional wrestling journalist covering multiple promotions is different from the average fan, the underlying issue applies broadly to wrestling consumption. Between Raw, SmackDown, and NXT, WWE produces 6-8 hours of content weekly. AEW generates roughly 4.5 to 5 hours when accounting for overruns. On PPV weekends, WWE adds 3 hours while AEW adds at least 5 hours. Even casually following a single promotion results in nearly or just about ten hours of wrestling per week, a dramatic increase from earlier eras when most fans watched only one hour of programming on syndication.

The editor recognizes that AEW President Tony Khan implements these extended broadcasts with good intentions, genuinely believing fans deserve maximum value for their investment. The editor respects Khan's passion for professional wrestling and his excitement about presenting shows filled with quality matches. However, the philosophy that extended content automatically equals better value doesn't resonate with everyone.

The core problem, according to the editor, centers on pacing. Every AEW match is seemingly constructed as a major bout requiring 15-20 minutes or more, which creates a relentless intensity that eventually becomes fatiguing. The editor references earlier five-hour NJPW shows that didn't feel excessively long because they incorporated smaller matches leading into main event attractions, allowing for natural breathing room. This contrasts sharply with AEW's approach where the mentality that everything and everyone must constantly go all out creates an unsustainable viewing experience.

The editor emphasizes this is not a criticism of AEW's wrestling quality. Recent booking decisions heading into All In have been questioned in previous coverage, but overall the publication respects AEW's product and fairly acknowledges that it features some of the best wrestling in the world. The issue isn't about quality deterioration or poor booking decisions across the board.

Rather, the complaint centers on the cumulative burden of increased volume. The editor states that by the time main events arrive, the experience shifts from exciting anticipation to relief that the show is ending. Rather than feeling eager about what happens next, the editor often simply wants AEW shows to conclude. The editor notes that beyond professional obligations, they have a personal life and desire to spend time outside.

The editor previously wrote about pacing as a separate issue and sees the current problem as an extension of that concern. In an era where wrestling content is more abundant than ever before, adding additional hours feels less like a gift to fans and more like an unwelcome chore. The mentality that constant escalation benefits audiences may actually be diminishing returns, particularly when fans are already struggling to keep up with the sheer volume of programming available across multiple promotions.
